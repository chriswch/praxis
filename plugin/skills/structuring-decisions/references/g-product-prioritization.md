### RICE Scoring Model (RICE) · fit 5
*aka / source:* Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort; introduced by Sean McBride at Intercom
- **What it is**: A prioritization formula invented by Intercom's growth team: RICE = (Reach × Impact × Confidence) ÷ Effort. Reach is the number of people/events affected per time period; Impact uses a fixed ordinal scale (3 = massive, 2 = high, 1 = medium, 0.5 = low, 0.25 = minimal); Confidence is a percentage (100% / 80% / 50%, with anything below 50% treated as a moonshot); Effort is measured in person-months. The score expresses total impact per unit of effort, used to rank a backlog.
- **Use in the process**: Score each candidate across the four columns and sort high-to-low as a starting point for discussion, not a verdict. When deciding, pay closest attention to the Confidence column: a low score signals "run discovery/an experiment first rather than build outright." Intercom is explicit that scores can be overridden by dependencies, sales table stakes, or strategic rationale — but the trade-off should be named when it happens.
- **Questions to ask**: It forces you to ask: how many actual *people* does this affect (versus "it feels important to me")? What empirical evidence backs this estimate (Confidence surfaces the data gap)? Is Effort estimated for the whole team or just the backend? An underestimated denominator is the most common blind spot.
- **Engineering**: Engineers should own the Effort column and part of Confidence: translate "does this need a migration in a multi-tenant setup, an Elasticsearch index change, or carry N+1 risk?" into person-months, so the PM doesn't use optimistic hours. Record the four sub-scores as custom fields in Jira/your ticketing system so technical-debt paydown competes against new features on the same yardstick.
- **Product**: Ranking a quarterly e-commerce SaaS roadmap — e.g., "auto-apply loyalty points at checkout" with Reach = monthly checkouts per merchant, Impact = conversion-rate lift, Confidence = whether A/B data exists, Effort = front- and back-end hours — compared on the same scale against "add a cart-replacement platform entry point."
- **Ops**: The Reach and Impact columns force you into product analytics (MAU, cohort conversion, funnels), replacing gut feel with dashboard numbers; afterward you can backfill actual Reach/Impact to calibrate the predictive accuracy of your RICE scores.
- **Strategy**: Best for ranking within a single comparable growth goal (one shared conversion metric); if candidates serve different strategic objectives, RICE mixes apples and oranges, so cluster first with a North Star / weighted scoring, then apply RICE within each cluster.
- **2026**: The 2025/2026 consensus is that RICE is for ranking within a chosen domain, not for cross-domain portfolio decisions. As AI drives Effort sharply down, some argue Confidence and kill conditions matter more than Effort; extensions that score human and agent workstreams separately have also appeared.
- Sources: https://www.intercom.com/blog/rice-simple-prioritization-for-product-managers/, https://www.productplan.com/glossary/rice-scoring-model, https://userpilot.com/blog/feature-prioritization-matrix/

### Jobs To Be Done (JTBD) · fit 5
*aka / source:* the jobs-to-be-done theory; popularized by Clayton Christensen (the milkshake case); roots traceable to Drucker (1954) and Levitt's "Marketing Myopia" (1960)
- **What it is**: The core question is: what job is the customer "hiring" this product to do? Christensen's milkshake case illustrates it — a fast-food chain found that morning milkshake buyers were not chasing flavor but a one-handed, long-lasting, boredom-killing commute companion; the real competitors were bananas and bagels, not other milkshakes. The unit of analysis is "the job in a situation," not product attributes or customer demographics.
- **Use in the process**: Before deciding whether to build something, ask which job it serves, in what situation, and against what it competes (possibly Excel, a rival, or even "doing nothing"). If a feature maps to no strong job, that is a signal not to build it.
- **Questions to ask**: What job is the customer hiring our product to do? What did they do last time to get this job done (which reveals the true competitor)? Does the same person hire us for the same reason across situations (morning vs. afternoon)? It shifts focus from "share of customer segment" to "share of situation."
- **Engineering**: Define acceptance criteria by the job rather than by feature spec, avoiding features that are technically correct but unused. When designing an API/abstraction, bound it by "the job the caller is trying to complete" rather than a feature list — this produces more stable interfaces.
- **Product**: An e-commerce SaaS "merchant" has several jobs: list products fast, stay up during peak season, understand which products sell, recover abandoned carts. JTBD helps you discover the real job a merchant hires the admin panel for, rather than piling on features. For example, a merchant is really hiring a report to "decide what to restock tomorrow," not to "look at numbers."
- **Ops**: Segment usage data by job/situation (not by feature module) to see which jobs are satisfied and which are merely "made do with." In funnel analysis, ask "what job is the user trying to complete when they get stuck?"
- **Strategy**: It defines the true competitive landscape and market boundaries (competitors may come from an entirely different category), grounding positioning and differentiation; it also guides growth into adjacent jobs.
- **2026**: JTBD has two main schools (Christensen's "situation/story" lens vs. Ulwick's ODI "measurable outcome" lens) that are often conflated and should be kept distinct. It remains a mainstream product-discovery mindset in 2025/2026, and AI tooling makes coding interview transcripts into jobs feasible at scale.
- Sources: https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/, https://therewiredgroup.com/case-studies/milkshakes/, https://online.hbs.edu/blog/post/jobs-to-be-done-examples

### Opportunity Solution Tree (OST) · fit 5
*aka / source:* OST; Teresa Torres, "Continuous Discovery Habits" (concept introduced in 2016)
- **What it is**: A visual tool from Teresa Torres with four top-down layers: (1) Outcome — a customer behavior/sentiment metric the team can directly influence, tied to business value; (2) Opportunity Space — unmet needs/pain points surfaced in interviews; (3) Solution Space — candidate solutions; (4) Assumption Tests — experiments that validate which solution creates both customer and business value.
- **Use in the process**: Anchor to one outcome to bound discovery, organize interview insights into the opportunity tree, make trade-offs first at the opportunity layer (assess opportunity sizing and market/company/customer factors, deliberately *not* estimating effort yet), and only after picking an opportunity do you ideate multiple solutions and use assumption tests to select the best — this is the build/don't-build path. Torres advises revisiting and pruning the tree every 3–4 interviews.
- **Questions to ask**: Which customer opportunity and which outcome does this solution actually connect to? (Prevents "solutions without a problem.") Are we comparing multiple solutions to the same opportunity, or comparing different opportunities? (Forces structured trade-offs.) Do we understand this opportunity well enough to generate at least three solutions?
- **Engineering**: As a member of the product trio, the engineer helps build the tree and injects technical feasibility and multiple implementation options early at the solution layer (rather than passively receiving requirements), and owns designing the smallest technical experiments that test assumptions (feature flags, fake doors, staged rollouts).
- **Product**: An e-commerce SaaS outcome such as "raise the share of merchants who complete their first shipment" expands into the merchant's shipping-flow opportunities (can't find the shipping label, can't make sense of convenience-store rules…); each opportunity spawns multiple solutions to experiment with, turning the roadmap from a "feature list" into structured exploration.
- **Ops**: The outcome must be a measurable behavior/sentiment metric, forcing the team to instrument and track it; opportunities come from qualitative interviews, producing a discovery rhythm where quantitative and qualitative data complement each other.
- **Strategy**: Connect high-level business goals (viability) down to customer needs (desirability) and then to solutions, aligning strategy with daily discovery and avoiding the "strategy deck vs. actual backlog" disconnect.
- **2026**: OST is a signature product-discovery framework of the 2020s and remains mainstream in 2025/2026. Torres stresses it serves continuous discovery (weekly customer contact) rather than one-off planning, and it is often paired with assumption mapping and JTBD.
- Sources: https://www.producttalk.org/opportunity-solution-trees/, https://www.productschool.com/blog/product-fundamentals/opportunity-solution-tree, https://andrewclark.co.uk/product-book-summaries/continuous-discovery-habits

### Assumption Mapping / Riskiest Assumption Test (RAT) · fit 5
*aka / source:* Assumptions Mapping; RAT (Riskiest Assumption Test); David J. Bland, "Testing Business Ideas" (Strategyzer)
- **What it is**: David Bland's three-step method: (1) extract assumptions — make explicit the desirability (customers want it), viability (it makes money), and feasibility (we can build it) assumptions underneath an idea; (2) plot them on an "importance × evidence" 2×2 (top-right = very important but least evidence = riskiest); (3) run the cheapest experiment against the riskiest assumption first.
- **Use in the process**: Before committing to build, decompose "this feature will succeed" into a chain of falsifiable assumptions, identify the one or two where "if it's wrong the whole idea collapses and we have the least certainty," and test those with the smallest experiment. The result directly drives build/pivot/kill — this is the standard way to turn a fuzzy idea into testable assumptions.
- **Questions to ask**: If this idea fails, which assumption is most likely the reason? Which critical assumption do we have the *least* evidence for? What is the cheapest, fastest experiment to test it instead of spending three months building? (Turns "I believe" into "I will test.")
- **Engineering**: Test assumptions with minimal technical investment before building: a fake landing page, Wizard of Oz (humans behind the scenes pretending to be automation), a feature flag rolled out to 5% of merchants, or even a single button to measure click-through. Avoid a full multi-tenant implementation for an unvalidated assumption.
- **Product**: Before shipping a new e-commerce feature (e.g., "AI auto-restock suggestions"), the riskiest assumption may be "merchants will trust and adopt the AI suggestion" rather than technical feasibility; run a semi-automated version with a few merchants to test adoption before committing heavily.
- **Ops**: Every assumption needs a falsifiable success threshold and a measurement method (conversion, adoption, retention), binding experiment design to analysis metrics so the results are trustworthy.
- **Strategy**: Before committing to a major new business/product line, systematically reduce the three risk classes (desirability/viability/feasibility), avoiding pouring resources into an unvalidated big bet; it operationalizes lean startup inside the enterprise.
- **2026**: Bland's "Testing Business Ideas" (Strategyzer) is the authoritative source, still mainstream in 2025/2026 and connecting directly to the assumption-test layer of OST. AI has slashed the cost of prototypes and fake pages, making "test before you build" cheaper and more obligatory.
- Sources: https://www.strategyzer.com/library/how-assumptions-mapping-can-focus-your-teams-on-running-experiments-that-matter, https://www.productcompass.pm/p/assumption-prioritization-canvas, https://www.humanizingwork.com/tackle-risk-with-david-bland/

### ICE Scoring (ICE) · fit 4
*aka / source:* Impact × Confidence × Ease; introduced by Sean Ellis (the father of growth hacking) at LogMeIn/Dropbox
- **What it is**: A fast scoring method Sean Ellis designed for growth experiments: ICE = Impact × Confidence × Ease, each scored 1–10 and multiplied. Lighter than RICE and purpose-built for rapid, iterative growth experiments that don't need rigorous effort estimation.
- **Use in the process**: Each person scores the growth-experiment backlog independently, then average or discuss the divergences and run the highest first. The point is not the absolute score but using score spreads to surface disagreement in the team's understanding (why did you give it 8 and I gave it 3).
- **Questions to ask**: If this experiment succeeds, how big is the Impact? Why do I believe it will succeed (Confidence forces out the evidence level)? How Easy is it to run? Multiplying the three naturally suppresses both "high impact but no certainty" and "certain but boring."
- **Engineering**: Suited to engineer-led growth/technical experiments — e.g., "add lazy loading to improve LCP" vs. "cache the checkout API," where Ease is judged by engineering and Confidence cites prior performance data. Faster than RICE and fit for within-sprint micro-decisions.
- **Product**: Ranking e-commerce SaaS onboarding/activation experiments (revamped first-run guidance, empty-state polish), which usually lack hard Reach numbers — ICE's subjective-but-fast nature suits them.
- **Ops**: The Confidence column can hook into Itamar Gilad's Confidence Meter (scoring by evidence level rather than gut), converting analytics data, user interviews, and market data into a confidence tier, mitigating ICE's biggest weakness — subjectivity.
- **2026**: ICE remains the most widespread framework among growth teams, but critics (e.g., Ward van Gasteren) note that multiplying three subjective 1–10 scores is mathematically fragile and easy to game. The 2025/2026 trend is to pair it with an explicit confidence-evidence scale.
- Sources: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-original-growth-hacker-sean-ellis, https://itamargilad.com/the-tool-that-will-help-you-choose-better-product-ideas/, https://growwithward.com/ice-prioritization-framework/

### Weighted Shortest Job First (WSJF) · fit 4
*aka / source:* WSJF; defined in SAFe; renamed CD3 (Cost of Delay Divided by Duration) by Black Swan Farming; rooted in Don Reinertsen's queueing theory
- **What it is**: WSJF = Cost of Delay ÷ Job Duration/Size. SAFe decomposes CoD as the sum of three relatively-estimated terms: user/business value, time criticality, and risk-reduction/opportunity-enablement (RR|OE). Its core claim is that *sequencing* — not computing per-item ROI — produces the best economic outcome in a flow system: among things of equal value, do the shortest first.
- **Use in the process**: For each feature/epic, estimate the three CoD terms relatively (often a modified Fibonacci scale), sum them, divide by a duration estimate, and schedule high-to-low. The insight: high-value-but-huge items get automatically pushed back, forcing you to slice thin to improve the numerator/denominator ratio.
- **Questions to ask**: What does delaying this by a month cost (translate "important" into time × money)? Does it have a time window — regulation or peak season — (Time Criticality)? What future work does it unlock (Opportunity Enablement)? Can we slice the big job small so it can ship first?
- **Engineering**: Especially useful for technical work — platform upgrades, debt paydown, and security patches often lack Reach but carry high RR|OE (risk reduction), and WSJF lets them compete fairly against new features. Engineers own the duration denominator and the risk-reduction term. Pre-peak-season performance hardening is a high-Time-Criticality case.
- **Product**: Cross-team scheduling of large multi-team epics, e.g., "integrate a new payment gateway" vs. "POS offline mode" — the three CoD terms surface the former's revenue urgency and the latter's risk reduction, avoiding decisions made on loudness alone.
- **Ops**: It requires quantifying CoD, forcing the team to estimate "if this feature slips a quarter, how much churn/unconverted revenue?" — which you can back out from existing revenue and churn data, anchoring the prioritization debate to economic numbers.
- **Strategy**: A portfolio-level economic sequencing tool that integrates value, urgency, and duration — well-suited for SaaS quarterly/half-year epic scheduling under constrained resources.
- **2026**: SAFe still treats WSJF as its flagship sequencing method in 2025/2026. Lightweight teams often find SAFe's three-term estimation too heavy and adopt the spirit of Black Swan Farming's CD3 instead (keeping just the CoD÷duration intuition). Reinertsen's view that cost of delay is "the golden key that unlocks many doors" remains the underlying theory.
- Sources: https://framework.scaledagile.com/wsjf, https://blackswanfarming.com/safe-and-weighted-shortest-job-first-wsjf/, https://www.productplan.com/glossary/weighted-shortest-job-first

### Cost of Delay (CoD) · fit 4
*aka / source:* CoD; CD3 (Cost of Delay Divided by Duration); Don Reinertsen, "The Principles of Product Development Flow"
- **What it is**: A concept from Don Reinertsen: the economic impact of time on an outcome — how much value is lost by delaying delivery over a period. It is the numerator of WSJF but can stand alone. CD3 = CoD ÷ duration, measuring the economic return per unit of time invested rather than total value.
- **Use in the process**: Reframe every decision from "should we do it" to "how much money is the difference between doing it now vs. three months later." When many things all "should" be done, use CoD to set the order; re-sequence when CoD shifts over time (e.g., around peak season).
- **Questions to ask**: How much are we actually paying for being slow? Is this delay a linear loss, or does it fall off a cliff past some point (missing peak season / a regulatory deadline)? Translate the "urgent vs. important" intuition into a comparable dollar amount.
- **Engineering**: Counter the "let's just leave this tech debt" instinct: quantify "not fixing this N+1 query costs X in monthly infrastructure plus Y in conversion loss." Investments in CI/CD and deployment speed can be argued via "the CoD savings of shorter lead time."
- **Product**: Go-to-market timing decisions: features before an e-commerce peak (Singles' Day / mid-year sale) carry extremely high, time-sensitive CoD — a one-week slip can cost a full quarter's revenue — and CoD turns this into a number rather than a feeling.
- **Ops**: Requires analyzing existing revenue curves, seasonality, and churn cost to estimate CoD; conversely, this is a valuable analytical exercise — it forces the team to build a "each day of delay = X dollars" model.
- **Strategy**: The theoretical foundation of all economic sequencing (WSJF/CD3), upgrading strategy debates from "which is important" to "whose delay is most expensive," which matters most when resources are scarce.
- **2026**: CoD is a time-tested first principle, still core to flow/lean thinking in 2025/2026. The commonly underappreciated point is that CoD changes over time and needs dynamic re-estimation rather than a one-time verdict.
- Sources: https://www.prodpad.com/glossary/cost-of-delay/, https://blackswanfarming.com/safe-and-weighted-shortest-job-first-wsjf/, https://wind4change.com/cost-delay-divided-duration-cd3-wsjf-reinertsen-safe/

### Kano Model (Kano) · fit 4
*aka / source:* Kano model; customer-satisfaction model; Noriaki Kano, 1980s
- **What it is**: Proposed by Noriaki Kano in the 1980s, holding that the relationship between "how much functionality" and "customer satisfaction" is nonlinear. Features fall into: Must-be/Basic (absence causes acute dissatisfaction, presence adds nothing), Performance/One-dimensional (more is more satisfying), Attractive/Delighter (absence doesn't penalize, presence delights), plus Indifferent (no effect) and Reverse (negative effect).
- **Use in the process**: Use the Kano questionnaire (a functional and a dysfunctional question per feature) to classify candidates. Decision principle: Must-be features are mandatory (the threshold); invest in Performance features against the competitive waterline; use Attractive features to differentiate but don't over-invest (they decay into Must-be over time).
- **Questions to ask**: Is this feature "we'll be criticized if it's missing" or "it delights if present"? Are we sinking resources into features customers are actually indifferent to (Indifferent)? Which of yesterday's delighters have become today's must-have threshold?
- **Engineering**: Distinguish basic-quality attributes like reliability/performance/security (good enough is enough; over-optimization has low marginal return) from genuinely differentiating features, guiding the allocation of engineering effort. For example, multi-tenant data isolation is a Must-be (a breach is catastrophic yet doing it well adds no delight), whereas AI auto-restock suggestions might be a delighter.
- **Product**: E-commerce SaaS feature planning: convenience-store pickup / payment-gateway integration are Must-be (their absence makes merchants churn outright); checkout speed is Performance; "AI smart product curation" and "one-click marketing-copy generation" might be delighters. Avoid pouring all resources into over-polishing Must-be features.
- **Ops**: Cross-analyze the Kano questionnaire with NPS/CSAT to identify which features genuinely drive satisfaction; track how fast delighters "lose their charm" (how long until they become Must-be) to time your reinnovation cadence.
- **Strategy**: At the heart of differentiation: what every competitor has is Must-be, so you pull ahead with delighters — but stay alert that delighters get matched by the market and become Must-be, requiring a continuous supply of new ones.
- **2026**: Kano is a 40-year classic that still holds. It is especially useful amid the 2025/2026 flood of AI features — many AI features are actually Indifferent or rapidly decaying from delighter to Must-be, and Kano helps you avoid blindly following the trend. Its main barrier is the cost of running the questionnaire.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kano_model, https://www.productplan.com/glossary/kano-model, https://asq.org/quality-resources/kano-model

### Opportunity Scoring / Outcome-Driven Innovation (ODI) · fit 4
*aka / source:* Outcome-Driven Innovation (ODI); importance vs. satisfaction; Tony Ulwick / Strategyn (1990s)
- **What it is**: Tony Ulwick operationalized JTBD: after interviews yield "desired outcomes," customers rate each outcome's importance and current satisfaction on 1–10, and the formula opportunity score = importance + max(importance − satisfaction, 0) (importance weighted double) surfaces "important but underserved" high-opportunity outcomes.
- **Use in the process**: Decide which improvement direction to invest in: high importance + low satisfaction = underserved, worth investing; high importance + high satisfaction = maintain; low importance = don't invest regardless of satisfaction. It gives "opportunity size" a comparable quantitative basis.
- **Questions to ask**: Which outcome do customers care about most yet are least satisfied with (that is the real opportunity gap)? Are we doubling down where customers are already satisfied (overserving)? Which outcomes do customers simply not care about?
- **Engineering**: Define requirements as outcome statements ("reduce the time to X," "lower the error rate of Y") rather than features, giving implementation measurable goals and acceptance criteria.
- **Product**: Survey-score e-commerce merchants' desired outcomes (e.g., "shorten the time to list one product," "reduce the number of missed shipments") to find the most painful yet least-satisfied steps as the next feature direction — avoiding things "we think are cool but merchants don't feel."
- **Ops**: The importance × satisfaction scatter plot is a powerful analytical asset; periodically re-test which opportunities new features have filled (satisfaction rising), quantifying the effect of product improvements.
- **Strategy**: ODI argues for segmenting markets by unmet outcomes rather than demographics, finding overlooked high-opportunity segments — a quantitative basis for growth and differentiation strategy.
- **2026**: ODI provides the measurability that JTBD lacks, but the survey design and outcome-statement extraction are demanding and costly, so small teams often borrow just the "importance vs. satisfaction" scatter lightly. Critics note its heavy reliance on respondent self-assessment.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outcome-Driven_Innovation, https://airfocus.com/glossary/what-is-opportunity-scoring/, https://innovationroundtable.com/summit/wp-content/uploads/2014/05/Strategyn_what_is_Outcome_Driven_Innovation.pdf

### Continuous Discovery · fit 4
*aka / source:* Continuous Discovery Habits; weekly customer contact; Teresa Torres
- **What it is**: A practice defined by Teresa Torres: the product trio responsible for the output contacts customers at least weekly and feeds the learning back into product decisions — rather than treating research as an occasional, one-off project. The goal is to make discovery the team's muscle memory.
- **Use in the process**: Institutionalize weekly interviews: every contact updates the OST and tests the latest assumptions, so build/don't-build decisions rest on continuously accumulated, recent customer evidence rather than a six-month-old report. The decision cadence stays in sync with the learning cadence.
- **Questions to ask**: When did we last talk to a real customer? (Reveals the freshness of the evidence behind a decision.) What is the most recent customer evidence behind this decision? Are we learning continuously, or doing one study and guessing for a year?
- **Engineering**: Engineers join interviews (even as observers) to build first-hand empathy and reduce spec ping-pong; they plan instrumentation/feature flags/staged rollouts as the infrastructure for continuous learning.
- **Product**: An e-commerce SaaS team interviews 1–2 merchants on a fixed weekly cadence, continuously correcting feature direction so that a big-investment decision like a "checkout redesign" keeps getting corrected by real feedback during development, instead of discovering it was wrong only after launch.
- **Ops**: Run qualitative (interviews) and quantitative (product analytics) in parallel: use data to spot anomalies, use interviews to understand causes, forming a continuous discovery flywheel.
- **Strategy**: Keeps strategy in sync with the market and adaptable, countering the rigidity of "annual planning that locks everything once"; it is one of the core capabilities of the product operating model.
- **2026**: In 2025/2026 it resonates strongly with Marty Cagan's product operating model. AI is being applied to interview transcription, coding, and insight extraction, lowering the operational cost of weekly contact and making continuous discovery more feasible.
- Sources: https://www.producttalk.org/opportunity-solution-trees/, https://gpavancitizen.medium.com/continous-discovery-habits-by-teresa-torres-63da0dea950, https://andrewclark.co.uk/product-book-summaries/continuous-discovery-habits

### User Story Mapping · fit 4
*aka / source:* Story Mapping; Jeff Patton, "User Story Mapping"; backbone & walking skeleton
- **What it is**: A two-dimensional backlog popularized by Jeff Patton: the horizontal axis is the sequence of activities/tasks in the user journey (the backbone, unsorted), and below each runs that task's user stories (sortable). This reveals the whole picture rather than a flat to-do list, and lets you carve out a "walking skeleton" — the thinnest end-to-end slice that actually works.
- **Use in the process**: In a workshop, lay out the journey backbone first, hang stories beneath each, then draw release-slice lines across: the first line is the walking skeleton/MVP — ensuring every release covers a minimal version of the complete journey rather than finishing one module before the next. Deciding which stories make this release is the build/don't-build call.
- **Questions to ask**: What steps does the user need to complete this whole thing? (Prevents missing a journey step.) Which thinnest end-to-end slice lets the user actually go through it once? (Forces a true MVP rather than a half-built piece.) If we cut these stories from this release, does the journey still work end to end?
- **Engineering**: Directly guides vertical-slice development and release planning, avoiding the horizontal trap of "finish the whole backend before the frontend"; the walking skeleton maps to the engineering practice of "wire up the minimal end-to-end happy path first," reducing integration risk.
- **Product**: Especially fitting for multi-step e-commerce flows like checkout/listing: arrange "add to cart → enter shipping → choose payment → pay → complete" as the backbone, slice the smallest version of each step for the first release to wire it through, then deepen each step (more payment methods, more carriers) in later releases.
- **Strategy**: Gives stakeholders a shared picture of "the complete user journey the product should solve," aligning vision and scope; a powerful tool for communicating "why we slice releases this way."
- **2026**: Story Mapping is a collaboration classic from the 2010s onward, with physical sticky notes largely migrated to online whiteboards like Miro/FigJam. Its "backbone + vertical slices + release slices" thinking pairs naturally with MVP, MoSCoW, and dual-track delivery.
- Sources: https://jpattonassociates.com/the-new-backlog/, https://www.avion.io/what-is-user-story-mapping/, https://www.samswerczek.com/blog/thinking-beyond-the-story-how-user-story-mapping-elevates-your-team-s-product-vision

### Lean Startup / Build-Measure-Learn · fit 4
*aka / source:* the BML loop; validated learning; Eric Ries, "The Lean Startup"
- **What it is**: Eric Ries's methodology, centered on the Build-Measure-Learn loop and validated learning: build an MVP with minimal investment → measure real customer behavior → learn and decide to persevere or pivot, with the goal of reducing uncertainty through scientific experiments and avoiding building something nobody wants.
- **Use in the process**: Treat every product decision as a hypothesis and design the smallest loop that produces validated learning: first ask "what do we need to learn," then reverse-engineer "the minimum we need to build." Decisions rely on actionable metrics (cohort retention, conversion) rather than vanity metrics (total sign-ups). The learning result drives the next build/pivot/kill.
- **Questions to ask**: What do we most need to *learn* right now to reduce the biggest uncertainty? (Reason backward from Learn, not forward from Build.) Could we learn the same thing with something smaller? Is this metric vanity or actionable? Persevere or pivot?
- **Engineering**: Guides "build the minimum learnable version" rather than full engineering: use feature flags, staged rollouts, and throwaway prototypes to shorten the loop; emphasize being measurable (instrumentation in place first) over code perfection and premature optimization.
- **Product**: A new e-commerce SaaS module starts from a minimum learnable version (a new marketing tool given to 20 merchants first, with humans filling in for automation), iterating on real usage data — avoiding building a big feature only to find merchants don't use it.
- **Ops**: The Measure stage is directly an analytics responsibility: build cohorts, actionable metrics, and an A/B testing framework; Ries specifically criticizes vanity metrics, pushing an analytics culture grounded in cohorts and conversion.
- **Strategy**: In highly uncertain new markets/products, it provides a "minimum cost for maximum learning" cadence; the pivot concept makes a strategic turn an evidence-based decision rather than a failure.
- **2026**: The key 2026 shift: AI makes "Build" extremely cheap (anyone can ship fast), so the bottleneck moves from "can we build it" to "should we, and how do we learn." Measure/Learn and the discipline of "don't rush to build" thus matter more (Strategyzer even argues "when you build-measure-learn, don't rush to build"). MVP is also often misused as "a rough complete product" rather than a learning tool.
- Sources: https://leanstartup.co/resources/articles/what-is-an-mvp/, https://userpilot.com/blog/build-measure-learn/, https://www.strategyzer.com/library/dont-build-when-you-build-measure-learn

### Minimum Viable Product (MVP) · fit 4
*aka / source:* MVP; coined by Frank Robinson, popularized by Eric Ries / Steve Blank
- **What it is**: Eric Ries's definition: the version of a product that yields the *maximum* validated learning about customers for the *least* effort. The point is learning, not minimalism — it is an experiment tool, not a quality-discounted complete product.
- **Use in the process**: First state which assumption this MVP must validate, then decide the minimum scope. Decision principle: defer anything beyond what's needed to validate that assumption. The MVP's result (threshold met/missed) decides whether to scale investment.
- **Questions to ask**: What question is this MVP meant to answer? (An MVP with no clear learning goal is just a half-built product.) To learn this, what is the least we need to build? Which features we assumed we'd need actually contribute nothing to learning and can be cut?
- **Engineering**: Corresponds to the walking skeleton — wire up the minimal end-to-end happy path rather than completing one layer. Often use concierge/Wizard of Oz (humans behind the scenes) to avoid building an automated system upfront, reserving engineering investment until validation holds.
- **Product**: Ship a new e-commerce SaaS feature to a small set of merchants first, deliberately keeping manual steps to test demand; only after adoption is validated do you invest in full automation and multi-tenant scale, reducing the risk of wasted work.
- **Ops**: An MVP must first define its success threshold and measurement, otherwise you can't judge persevere vs. pivot; this forces the team to clarify "what success looks like" before building.
- **Strategy**: Lowers the initial investment and risk of a new direction, letting strategy "place a small bet, watch the response, then double down" — a low-risk scout for new markets/segments.
- **2026**: MVP is one of the most badly misused terms — often taken as "a stripped-down complete product." In 2025/2026 some switch to MLP (Minimum Lovable Product) or RAT (test only the riskiest assumption, building no product at all) as a corrective; AI has slashed prototyping cost, so MVPs should be used to learn rather than to rush to scale.
- Sources: https://leanstartup.co/resources/articles/what-is-an-mvp/, https://www.strategyzer.com/library/dont-build-when-you-build-measure-learn, https://userpilot.com/blog/build-measure-learn/

### Dual-Track Agile · fit 4
*aka / source:* Dual-Track Scrum; Discovery + Delivery dual tracks; Marty Cagan & Jeff Patton (2012); Cagan now reframes as Continuous Discovery/Delivery
- **What it is**: One team runs two parallel tracks: the Discovery track (use research, prototypes, and interviews to find *what to build* and validate opportunity size) and the Delivery track (build the validated thing right, well, and ship it). Discovery's output is Delivery's input. Marty Cagan and Jeff Patton framed it as "dual-track scrum" in 2012.
- **Use in the process**: Split "whether to build" (discovery) from "how to build it well" (delivery) organizationally while keeping the same team collaborating: only items that pass discovery validation enter the delivery backlog, mechanically blocking "building before validating." Decisions thus pass two gates: an opportunity-validation gate and a delivery-scope gate.
- **Questions to ask**: Has this item entering the delivery track been validated in the discovery track? (Prevents scheduling unvalidated assumptions straight into a sprint.) Does our discovery feed delivery's pace? (The two cadences must match, or one starves or jams.)
- **Engineering**: Engineers participate in both tracks: on delivery, focus on quality and stability; on discovery, contribute feasibility assessments and quick prototypes. Clarify that throwaway prototypes (discovery) and production code (delivery) have different quality bars, so prototypes aren't shipped directly.
- **Product**: Each sprint an e-commerce SaaS team simultaneously: delivers validated features and runs discovery (interviewing merchants + prototyping to validate the next batch of opportunities), keeping the roadmap stocked with "validated, ready-to-build" items rather than starting to think about the next thing only after finishing development.
- **Ops**: The discovery track leans heavily on qualitative interviews and quick data experiments; the delivery track relies on post-launch outcome metrics — together forming a complete learning loop.
- **Strategy**: Ensures the organization both "builds the right thing" and "builds the thing right," avoiding two common failures: efficiently building something nobody wants, or slowly building the right thing.
- **2026**: A notable evolution: since "INSPIRED" 2nd edition, Marty Cagan has dropped the term "dual-track agile" because it makes people over-focus on process rather than principles, switching to Continuous Discovery and Continuous Delivery. In 2025/2026 it is mostly folded into his product operating model.
- Sources: https://www.svpg.com/dual-track-agile/, https://blog.logrocket.com/product-management/dual-track-agile-continuous-discovery/, https://www.productboard.com/glossary/dual-track-agile/

### Product Trio · fit 4
*aka / source:* Product Trio; PM + Designer + Engineer; Teresa Torres / Marty Cagan (empowered product team)
- **What it is**: The smallest unit of continuous discovery: a product manager, a designer, and an engineer (tech lead) jointly own an outcome and an opportunity space and make discovery decisions together. The three perspectives (desirability/feasibility/viability) are at the table from the start, rather than handed off sequentially.
- **Use in the process**: Have the trio make build/don't-build discovery decisions together, rather than the PM deciding alone and handing off: the engineer enters early to assess feasibility, the designer brings the user's view, and the three make trade-offs together on the OST and assumption tests, raising both decision quality and buy-in.
- **Questions to ask**: Are all three perspectives (do they want it / can we build it / does it make money) present for this decision? Is the engineer told only at the end, or shaping the solution from the discovery stage? Are we doing a handoff or co-creating?
- **Engineering**: The engineer is promoted from "requirement receiver" to "joint discovery decision-maker," able to inject technical feasibility at the earliest stage, propose cheaper alternatives, and catch multi-tenant/performance risks early — sharply reducing late rework and spec ping-pong.
- **Product**: Each e-commerce SaaS product team is staffed with a trio jointly owning an outcome like "raise the success rate of merchant self-serve listing," so feature design accounts for usability, feasibility, and profitability from day one.
- **Strategy**: A structural cornerstone of Cagan's empowered teams and the product operating model — shifting teams from "feature factories" to "teams empowered to achieve outcomes."
- **2026**: In 2025/2026 it has become a mainstream organizational unit as the product operating model spreads. With AI, there is discussion that the trio's work will be AI-assisted (automated interview analysis, prototype generation), but the core structure of "three perspectives deciding together" still holds.
- Sources: https://www.producttalk.org/opportunity-solution-trees/, https://blog.logrocket.com/product-management/dual-track-agile-continuous-discovery/, https://userpilot.medium.com/moving-to-the-product-operating-model-by-marty-cagan-1e45b8e4f414

### North Star Framework · fit 4
*aka / source:* North Star Metric (NSM) + Input Metrics; Amplitude / John Cutler, "The North Star Playbook"
- **What it is**: A model popularized by Amplitude (John Cutler co-authored the Playbook): pick a single North Star Metric (NSM) representing the value the product delivers to customers, with a few input metrics beneath it that the team can directly influence; the team moves the NSM by moving its inputs. Amplitude's own NSM is WLU (Weekly Learning Users).
- **Use in the process**: Use the NSM and its inputs as the compass for prioritization: ask of every candidate feature, "which input does it move, and does that move the NSM?" Anything that connects to no input is a low-priority signal. It aligns scattered feature decisions to one shared value goal.
- **Questions to ask**: Which input metric can this feature move? (If none, be wary.) Does our NSM really represent the value customers receive, or is it just a proxy for revenue? (Cutler: "if you can move the NSM directly, it probably isn't a good NSM" — it should be reachable but a layer removed.)
- **Engineering**: Aligns engineering investment to measurable impact: technical work can also argue "cut page load → lift an input (checkout completion rate) → move the NSM." It also guides instrumentation design — first ensure the NSM and inputs can be correctly tracked and measured.
- **Product**: An e-commerce SaaS might pick "weekly successfully shipped orders per merchant" as the NSM, with inputs (active merchants × products listed per merchant × conversion rate × fulfillment success rate), each product team owning one input as its roadmap focus.
- **Ops**: The NSM + inputs form the skeleton of dashboards and OKRs, replacing vanity metrics with a value proxy plus influenceable levers; the inputs often form a flywheel, so analysis should look at their mutually reinforcing effects.
- **Strategy**: Gives the whole organization a single shared definition of "what winning means," linking daily feature decisions to long-term strategic value; Cutler stresses the NSM is the combined result of cross-functional collaboration, deliberately designed so no single team can directly game it.
- **2026**: In 2025/2026 it remains the mainstream alignment metric for SaaS. Common traps: using revenue as the NSM (that is an outcome, not a value proxy), or setting an NSM a single team can directly game. In the AI-product era there is discussion of NSMs centered on value actually being used/consumed (Amplitude's WLU stresses learning being consumed by others).
- Sources: https://amplitude.com/books/north-star/about-north-star-framework, https://info.amplitude.com/rs/138-CDN-550/images/Amplitude-The-North-Star-Playbook.pdf, https://imanageproducts.com/producthead-john-cutlers-north-star-metric/

### MoSCoW Method · fit 3
*aka / source:* Must/Should/Could/Won't have; introduced by Dai Clegg at Oracle (1994), adopted by DSDM
- **What it is**: A classification introduced by Dai Clegg at Oracle in 1994 and widely adopted by DSDM, sorting requirements into four classes: Must have (without it, it's not a delivery), Should have (important but not fatal), Could have (nice to have), Won't have (explicitly not this time). The lowercase o's are just for pronunciation.
- **Use in the process**: Used to negotiate delivery scope under a fixed deadline/fixed resources: lock the Musts (commonly advised to stay under ~60% of total effort) and treat the rest as buffer — when behind schedule, cut Coulds first, then Shoulds, never the Musts. Write the Won'ts down explicitly to prevent scope creep.
- **Questions to ask**: If we could deliver only one thing before the deadline, which is "fail if not delivered"? Have we stuffed too much into Must (>60% means we aren't making trade-offs)? What are we explicitly not doing this time (Won't prevents smuggling)?
- **Engineering**: Sprint/release scope cutting and MVP definition: map the release's Musts to the necessary paths of the walking skeleton, and treat Coulds as polish only if time allows. Especially practical for features with a fixed launch date (e.g., tied to a marketing window).
- **Product**: E-commerce feature release scoping: for a new checkout flow, "payment can be charged successfully" is a Must, "loyalty points auto-applied" is a Should, "checkout-page animation" is a Could, and "multi-currency" is a Won't this time — giving a clear cut order under deadline pressure.
- **2026**: MoSCoW is simple but cannot express the relative order *within* the Musts, and is often misused by marking everything Must. A common 2025/2026 combination: use RICE/WSJF for cross-domain ranking, and MoSCoW only as the final scope-trade-off gate within a delivery deadline.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MoSCoW_method, https://www.productplan.com/glossary/moscow-prioritization, https://userpilot.com/blog/feature-prioritization-matrix/

### Value vs. Effort Matrix · fit 3
*aka / source:* Impact-Effort Matrix; Quick Wins / Big Bets / Fill-ins / Time Sinks quadrants
- **What it is**: Plot candidates on a 2×2 by value (vertical) and effort (horizontal), forming four quadrants: Quick Wins (high value, low effort — prioritize), Big Bets (high value, high effort — strategic bets), Fill-ins (low value, low effort — do when you have time), Time Sinks (low value, high effort — avoid).
- **Use in the process**: In a workshop, stick notes into quadrants to quickly form consensus. Decision principle: harvest Quick Wins first, pick a few Big Bets carefully, and say no to Time Sinks outright. A healthy backlog is roughly 30–40% Quick Wins and 20–30% Big Bets.
- **Questions to ask**: Is this really a Quick Win, or have we underestimated the multi-tenant complexity (the effort axis is routinely estimated optimistically)? Do we have too many Big Bets we're afraid to cut? Which are Time Sinks dressed up as value?
- **Engineering**: Engineers should guard the honesty of the effort axis — a front-end feature that looks simple may be a Big Bet under multi-tenancy + permissions + data migration. Tech-debt paydown can also go on the chart, often landing in Quick Win (low effort, high long-term value) where it becomes visible.
- **Product**: The first coarse screen of new ideas, filtering out obvious Time Sinks before it's worth running RICE, so you focus on the few items worth deeper evaluation.
- **Strategy**: Helps the team balance "steadily harvesting Quick Wins to maintain momentum" against "placing a few Big Bets for differentiation," a good tool for communicating strategic cadence.
- **2026**: Its strengths are speed, visual clarity, and ease of communication; its weakness is having only two axes and being highly subjective, with critics noting it easily dresses "gut feel" up as "analysis" (some articles flatly call it "obsolete"). In 2025/2026 it is generally used as an early coarse screen, with formal ranking still handed to RICE/WSJF.
- Sources: https://www.savio.io/product-roadmap/value-vs-effort-matrix/, https://miro.com/templates/2x2-prioritization-matrix/, https://www.claytonkjos.com/blog/6-major-flaws-that-make-the-impact-vs-effort-matrix-obsolete
