> Decision-Science Method Catalog · E. Cognitive biases & debiasing · 17 methods. Legend: engineering / product / ops / strategy = four-axis application; fit = fit with software/SaaS (3–5).

### Dual-Process Thinking (System 1 / System 2) · fit 5
*aka / source:* Dual-process theory; Thinking, Fast and Slow (Kahneman 2011); the underlying distinction originated with Stanovich & West and was popularized by Kahneman
- **What it is**: In Thinking, Fast and Slow, Kahneman distinguishes two modes of cognition. System 1 is fast, automatic, emotional, and unconscious (intuitive reaction); System 2 is slow, effortful, logical, and deliberately invoked (analytic deliberation). Most biases arise when System 1 takes a heuristic shortcut and System 2 is never engaged to check it.
- **Use in the process**: Triage decisions. Let System 1 / intuition handle low-stakes, reversible, high-frequency choices quickly (don't over-analyze); deliberately switch to System 2 for high-stakes, irreversible, expensive ones (write down assumptions, seek disconfirming evidence, run the data). Build triggers: when a decision meets certain conditions (affects multiple tenants, changes schema, touches money flows), force it onto the slow path (design review, RFC).
- **Questions to ask**: "Is this an intuition or a conclusion I reasoned to?" "Is this decision worth engaging System 2, or am I over-thinking a two-way-door decision?" "Could my current judgment just be an illusion produced by familiarity or fluency?"
- **Engineering**: In code review, a familiar pattern lets System 1 wave it through ("looks right"); for security-sensitive diffs (SQL, permissions, tenant isolation) deliberately switch to System 2 and verify line by line rather than scanning on intuition. Be especially alert with LLM-generated code — fluent code is the most likely to trip System 1 into approving it.
- **Product**: When evaluating a request, "this feature sounds reasonable" is System 1; the System 2 move is to ask whose JTBD it solves, how many tenants would use it, and what the opportunity cost is. Treat intuition as a hypothesis, not a conclusion.
- **Ops**: Looking at a dashboard, a spike instantly gets a causal story (System 1); deliberately stop and ask whether it's seasonality, a single large-tenant event, or insufficient sample size (System 2).
- **Strategy**: In strategy meetings, "everyone feels X is right" is usually System 1 collective fluency; force major bets through a System 2 process (pre-mortem, outside view).
- **2026**: 2025–2026 research (e.g. arXiv 2403.00811, 2601.08045) finds LLMs also exhibit System-1-like biases (anchoring, confirmation), and developer trust in fluent AI output suppresses their own System 2 checking. Requiring the LLM to produce step-by-step reasoning and several alternatives can serve as a forced System 2 intervention.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thinking,_Fast_and_Slow, https://thedecisionlab.com/reference-guide/philosophy/system-1-and-system-2-thinking, https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.00811

### Sunk Cost Fallacy · fit 5
*aka / source:* Escalation of commitment; Concorde fallacy
- **What it is**: Already-spent, unrecoverable costs (time, effort, money) irrationally drive a current decision — "we've come this far, quitting would waste it all." A rational decision should compare only the future benefits and costs of each option from now on; sunk costs should be ignored.
- **Use in the process**: Strike already-spent costs from the discussion and ask only: if I started from zero today, knowing everything I now know, would I still choose this path? Set pre-committed kill criteria and checkpoints so stopping becomes an institution rather than a personal admission of loss.
- **Questions to ask**: "If this project were inherited from someone else and I had no emotional investment, would I continue?" "Are we continuing because it will succeed, or because we've already invested so much?" "What is the opportunity cost of continuing?"
- **Engineering**: Two years spent building an in-house CMS / framework that you know is buggy and slows the team, yet you refuse to switch to a mature option because "we invested two years" — a textbook sunk cost. The same applies to a complex hand-rolled abstraction or a half-finished microservice migration: use version control so that "rewrite from scratch" is a controlled, low-regret cost.
- **Product**: A low-usage feature that took heavy engineering to ship, then keeps drawing maintenance instead of being retired; build a feature-sunset mechanism, the way Google routinely shuts down products (the Google Graveyard).
- **Ops**: A campaign or marketing channel with ad budget already spent but negative ROI, kept running because "we've already paid" — judge only on marginal ROI. Use cohort data to set a stop-loss line.
- **Strategy**: For a product line with heavy accumulated investment (e.g. an in-house POS hardware integration), a strategy review must explicitly exclude the accumulated investment from the "continue or not" judgment.
- **2026**: A new form appears in the LLM era: a developer has tuned a particular prompt / agent pipeline and won't switch approaches even when results are poor (suggester preference plus sunk cost). Use cheap A/B comparisons across several approaches to lower the psychological barrier to abandoning one.
- Sources: https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/the-sunk-cost-fallacy, https://www.equalexperts.com/blog/our-thinking/how-to-avoid-sunk-cost-fallacy-in-software-projects/, https://lawsofsoftwareengineering.com/laws/sunk-cost-fallacy/

### Anchoring · fit 5
*aka / source:* Anchoring-and-adjustment heuristic (Tversky & Kahneman 1974)
- **What it is**: The first number you see becomes an anchor, and subsequent judgments drift toward it with insufficient adjustment, even when that number is irrelevant or random. Lovallo & Kahneman's HBR "Delusions of Success" identifies anchoring as a major driver of overestimated project benefits.
- **Use in the process**: Before group estimation or negotiation, prevent anyone from throwing out a number first. Have everyone estimate independently and reveal simultaneously; surface the range of views before discussing. Stay alert to externally supplied "target numbers" (a manager's expectation, a customer's quote) and probe their basis.
- **Questions to ask**: "Is my estimate being dragged along by that number I just heard?" "Would my answer differ if the first person had said a different number?" "Where did this benchmark number come from, and is it credible?"
- **Engineering**: Planning Poker is designed to be anti-anchoring: members pick cards independently and reveal simultaneously, and the PO states the requirement without floating a point value, so a senior engineer's first utterance doesn't anchor the whole team's estimate. A pre-filled hour estimate on a ticket, or the points on the previous similar JIRA ticket, are implicit anchors too.
- **Product**: In pricing or roadmap capacity planning, the numbers in the first-draft will anchor the entire discussion; have stakeholders write down their own first, then aggregate.
- **Ops**: When setting KPI targets, last year's number anchors this year's target and ignores structural market change; a report's default time range or comparison baseline also anchors interpretation.
- **Strategy**: In M&A / investment valuation or competitor-pricing comparisons, the first number the other side floats anchors the whole negotiation; compute your own independent range from an outside view beforehand.
- **2026**: 2025–2026 research finds LLMs are strongly anchored too: an irrelevant number in the prompt can materially shift their output (e.g. a recommended "sentence length," a story-point estimate). When estimating with AI, don't pre-load your own expected number into the prompt, and reorder the options before comparing results to detect anchoring.
- Sources: https://hbr.org/2003/07/delusions-of-success-how-optimism-undermines-executives-decisions, https://www.eficode.com/blog/make-effort-estimates-you-can-actually-trust-by-removing-anchoring-bias, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.10124

### Planning Fallacy · fit 5
*aka / source:* Optimism bias in planning; introduced by Kahneman & Tversky 1979
- **What it is**: A systematic underestimation of time, cost, and risk together with overestimation of benefits. The root cause is taking the inside view — looking only at the specifics of this case rather than the historical distribution of outcomes across similar cases.
- **Use in the process**: For any schedule or estimate, force an outside-view comparison: how long did past work of this kind actually take? Multiply the "ideal-path estimate" by a historical overrun factor. When committing a delivery date externally, add a data-grounded buffer rather than a gut feel.
- **Questions to ask**: "How long did past similar migrations / features actually take, as opposed to how long I hope this one takes?" "Am I only picturing the happy path and ignoring time for integration, code review, and release rollback?"
- **Engineering**: Chronic underestimation in sprint pointing is a textbook planning fallacy; use the team's historical velocity and "how long similar epics actually took" as the outside view rather than re-estimating each card optimistically. Large refactors and DB migrations suffer most.
- **Product**: A new feature "shippable in two weeks" routinely becomes two months; calibrate roadmap commitments against the lead-time distribution (reference class) of past features.
- **Ops**: For data-migration and reporting-rebuild projects, build an internal database of project durations to enable reference-class forecasting.
- **Strategy**: For the time and cost of entering a new market or launching a new product line, Lovallo & Kahneman note that most major business initiatives ultimately fall short of expectations; bake the outside view into strategic planning.
- **2026**: "AI will speed up development" is itself manufacturing a fresh round of planning fallacy — teams assume Copilot lets them cut the schedule while ignoring the hidden cost of verifying AI code and fixing hallucinations (arXiv 2601.08045 finds 48.8% of development actions carry a bias). Still calibrate against actual lead-time data.
- Sources: https://whennotesfly.com/concepts/psychology-behavior/planning-fallacy-explained, https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/nobel-project-management-reference-class-forecasting-8068, https://corporate.jasoncollins.blog/outside-view

### Confirmation Bias · fit 5
*aka / source:* Myside bias; confirmatory tendency
- **What it is**: A tendency to seek, interpret, and remember information that supports an existing belief while ignoring or discounting disconfirming evidence. In data analysis it shows up as cherry-picking and as "peeking at A/B results and calling it early."
- **Use in the process**: Before deciding, write down your hypothesis and "what evidence would falsify it" (falsification conditions). Deliberately assign someone to find disconfirming evidence (see red team). Pre-register the A/B test's hypothesis, sample size, success criteria, and run length, and don't peek or stop early before it ends.
- **Questions to ask**: "What data would prove me wrong, and have I gone looking for it?" "Am I testing a hypothesis, or finding reasons to support what I already want to do?" "Have I looked at the segments / metrics that don't support me?"
- **Engineering**: While debugging, fixating on the cause "I believe in," collecting only logs that support it and ignoring contradicting evidence (arXiv 2601.08045 labels this CB1 Belief Confirmation); use bisection / a minimal reproduction rather than only confirming the preferred hypothesis. LLMs will also play along with the assumptions embedded in your prompt, amplifying confirmation bias.
- **Product**: After building the feature you wanted, interviewing only the users who like it and reading only positive feedback; deliberately seek out churned users and look at counter-indicators. Amplitude flags confirmation bias as a common PM trap.
- **Ops**: A/B "peeking": stopping the moment the result turns positive and declaring victory is a statistically invalid form of confirmation bias. Fix the run length and use a pre-set significance threshold. Don't report only the segments that support the conclusion either.
- **Strategy**: For an established strategic direction, gathering only the bullish market signals; use a red team or an outside perspective to force a confrontation with the bearish case.
- **2026**: LLM sycophancy reinforces a user's confirmation bias — the way you ask shapes the agreeable answer it gives back. 2025 research suggests metacognitive prompting ("where might I be wrong?") to make the AI actively list the opposing arguments.
- Sources: https://amplitude.com/blog/confirmation-bias, https://www.ezbot.ai/post/five-cognitive-biases-that-can-skew-your-a-b-testing-decisions-and-how-to-avoid-them, https://arxiv.org/html/2601.08045v1

### Overconfidence Bias · fit 5
*aka / source:* Overprecision / overestimation / overplacement (three forms)
- **What it is**: Overestimating your own knowledge, ability, and predictive accuracy while underestimating the uncertainty of the world. It afflicts experts and laypeople alike; in estimation it shows up as confidence intervals that are too narrow (overprecision). It is fed by hindsight bias and the illusion of certainty.
- **Use in the process**: Estimate with ranges rather than point values, and deliberately widen them (ask: what is your 90% confidence interval? It is usually still too narrow). Record forecast accuracy for calibration training. For major decisions, require confidence calibration: how much would you bet on it?
- **Questions to ask**: "How sure am I — would I bet on it?" "Is my confidence interval too narrow? How far apart are the worst and best cases?" "What makes me think I'm more accurate than the historical base rate?"
- **Engineering**: Engineers give single-point hour estimates ("about 3 days") rather than ranges, and the ranges they do give are too narrow; "this change is safe, no need for much testing" is a high-frequency overconfidence phrase. Require three-point estimates (optimistic / most likely / pessimistic).
- **Product**: A PM overconfident that "users will surely love this feature," skipping validation and rolling out to everyone; validate the hypothesis on a small traffic slice first.
- **Ops**: Over-trusting a forecasting model's point estimate while ignoring the prediction interval; always report a forecast with its uncertainty band.
- **Strategy**: Lovallo & Kahneman note that over-optimism plus overconfidence make most major business initiatives fall short; calibrate strategic bets with the outside view and a pre-mortem.
- **2026**: AI-assisted development amplifies overconfidence: an LLM delivers a possibly hallucinated answer in a highly confident register, and the developer's automation bias means they stop checking it (arXiv 2601.08045 calls this suggester preference). Force "verification warnings / productive friction" onto AI output.
- Sources: https://www.scribbr.com/research-bias/overconfidence-bias/, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.00125, https://www.masterclass.com/articles/overconfidence-bias

### Authority Bias / HiPPO · fit 5
*aka / source:* Highest Paid Person's Opinion (named by Kaushik & Kohavi 2006); Authority bias
- **What it is**: A decision dominated by the intuition of the most senior / highest-paid person in the room, overriding the data and the judgment of frontline experts. HiPPO was introduced by Avinash Kaushik and Ronny Kohavi in 2006; the core problem is deciding "on gut feel rather than evidence," and that person is often the furthest from the customer.
- **Use in the process**: Make data and experiments the arbiter: use A/B tests to turn "who decides" into "the data decides." In meetings, let the most junior speak first and have the manager weigh in last (to avoid anchoring). Record "whose opinion this is" separately from "what the evidence is."
- **Questions to ask**: "Is this decision grounded in data or in someone's intuition?" "If this opinion came from someone other than the boss, would we still do it?" "Can we settle this disagreement with an experiment?"
- **Engineering**: Architecture / technology choices dominated by a senior staff engineer's preference, with no spike / benchmark data; use an RFC plus a measured prototype so the argument rests on evidence rather than rank.
- **Product**: The CEO's offhand "I think users want X" jumps the roadmap queue; the usual advice is to counter the HiPPO with A/B tests and user data. A multi-tenant SaaS should read cross-tenant data rather than the preferences of one large customer's boss.
- **Ops**: When interpreting data, a manager's predetermined conclusion drives the attribution; the analyst should pre-register metric definitions and hypotheses so the conclusion can be falsified by data.
- **Strategy**: A major strategy decided by a founder's gut while ignoring market data; Bezos's "disagree and commit" is, conversely, healthy HiPPO management — a leader may push ahead but must state explicitly that it's a bet.
- **2026**: An "AI HiPPO" has emerged — treating an LLM's output as authoritative (automation bias) and ceasing to question it. The target of authority bias has expanded from "the boss" to "the AI." The countermeasures are the same: demand evidence, run experiments, and keep humans accountable for checking.
- Sources: https://exp-platform.com/hippo/, https://www.fivetran.com/blog/taming-the-hippo-with-data-driven-decision-making, https://uservoice.com/blog/highest-paid-persons-opinion

### Reference-Class Forecasting / Outside View · fit 5
*aka / source:* RCF; Outside view (Kahneman & Tversky; Lovallo & Kahneman 2003; extended to infrastructure by Flyvbjerg)
- **What it is**: The core method against the planning fallacy and over-optimism: rather than estimating from a project's internal details (inside view), find a set of comparable completed projects (the reference class) to build an outcome distribution, then locate this project within that distribution. The UK and Denmark have made it mandatory for public investment appraisal.
- **Use in the process**: Three steps: (1) identify a reference class of similar projects; (2) obtain that class's actual outcome distribution (duration / cost / success rate); (3) use the distribution to correct this project's inside estimate (usually adjusting it up). Archive every project's actuals to accumulate a reference dataset over time.
- **Questions to ask**: "What does the actual outcome distribution of the last N similar projects look like?" "What makes us think we'll land on the good end of the distribution rather than the median?" "Do I have both an inside-view and an outside-view estimate, and how far apart are they?"
- **Engineering**: To estimate migration / refactor effort, don't re-estimate optimistically — look up the actual lead-time distribution of the team's past work of similar scale. Build an engineering-actuals database (epic estimate vs. actual) for later calibration.
- **Product**: For a new feature's launch timeline / adoption rate, reference the actual adoption curves of past comparable features rather than treating each as "this time is different."
- **Ops**: When building a growth / revenue forecast, use the historical distribution of comparable merchants / comparable campaigns (the reference class) as the baseline; there is published research using reference-class forecasting for corporate revenue-growth prediction.
- **Strategy**: When entering a new market or launching a new product line, first look at the base-rate success of comparable industry moves (most fall short of expectations) to calibrate the optimistic assumptions in the business plan.
- **2026**: RCF and AI are highly complementary: an LLM / ML model can rapidly build a reference class from a large body of historical projects and produce the distribution. 2024–2025 work on distributional reference-class forecasting combines multiple reference variables for more precise prediction.
- Sources: https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/nobel-project-management-reference-class-forecasting-8068, https://hbr.org/2003/07/delusions-of-success-how-optimism-undermines-executives-decisions, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.03402

### Pre-mortem · fit 5
*aka / source:* Prospective hindsight (Mitchell, Russo & Pennington 1989); practical method introduced by Gary Klein, HBR 2007
- **What it is**: Before a project kicks off, assume "a year from now it has failed completely" and have the team work backwards to write down why. It exploits prospective hindsight — research finds that imagining an event has already happened improves the ability to correctly identify reasons for future outcomes by roughly 30%. It counters overconfidence, groupthink, and confirmation bias.
- **Use in the process**: At kickoff, the leader announces "assume the project is dead — write down the cause of death"; everyone writes independently (to avoid anchoring and conformity), then the causes are aggregated and ranked, turning the high-probability / high-impact ones into a mitigation plan. Kahneman's personal version: before a major decision, imagine that a year from now it turned out to be a mistake.
- **Questions to ask**: "Assume this project fails badly next year — what's the most likely reason?" "What risks are we currently afraid to say out loud?" "Which assumption, if wrong, would collapse the whole thing?"
- **Engineering**: Run a pre-mortem before a large release / migration / major architecture change: imagine post-release data corruption or a full-site outage, and reason backward to the likely paths (rollback failure, dependency-service timeout, a hole in tenant isolation), then harden in advance and rehearse the rollback.
- **Product**: Run a pre-mortem before a new feature's GA: assume that three months out its usage is zero or it has generated a flood of complaints, identify the likely causes (didn't solve a real need, onboarding too complex, performance drag), and adjust accordingly.
- **Ops**: Before a major data migration / metric refactor, envision the "reports are all wrong, leading to bad decisions" cause of death, and set up reconciliation and monitoring in advance.
- **Strategy**: Run a pre-mortem before a major strategic bet so voices afraid to challenge the consensus have a safe channel to surface risk — directly countering groupthink and the HiPPO.
- **2026**: Since 2025 it's common to use an LLM as a "cheap pre-mortem partner": have the AI play a critical red team that lists failure scenarios. Beware AI sycophancy, though, and prompt explicitly: "play the most pessimistic critic and find 10 reasons this will fail."
- Sources: https://nesslabs.com/pre-mortem-anticipate-failure-with-prospective-hindsight, https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/hbr-guide-to/9781422143339/OEBPS/Text/07.html, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-mortem

### Red Team / Devil's Advocate · fit 5
*aka / source:* Red Team–Blue Team; the devil's advocate originates with the Vatican's Office of the Devil's Advocate
- **What it is**: Deliberately and institutionally injecting an opposing viewpoint to root out groupthink and confirmation bias. The devil's advocate appoints a designated person to challenge the consensus; red-team / blue-team splits two independent groups to argue for and against. The key is to make "challenging" a formal, sanctioned role rather than personal nitpicking.
- **Use in the process**: For major decisions, appoint (on rotation) a devil's advocate whose job is to attack the proposal. Or split into two teams that prepare the for / against cases and then debate. Give dissent institutional authority, lowering the social cost to the person raising it.
- **Questions to ask**: "If I had to convince others this decision is wrong, what's the strongest argument?" "How would the opposition attack our most prized assumption?" "Has the harmonious atmosphere kept anyone from daring to object?"
- **Engineering**: In a design review / RFC, assign one reviewer specifically to find the architecture's fatal weaknesses (security, scalability, multi-tenant data isolation); a security review is essentially a red team. For AI-generated code, have another agent / human act as the red team to vet it rather than accepting it one-directionally.
- **Product**: In a roadmap review, appoint a devil's advocate to challenge the assumptions behind the most favored feature, countering the PM's confirmation bias and the HiPPO.
- **Ops**: Before delivering an important analytical conclusion, ask another analyst to specifically challenge its data-slicing, attribution, and alternative explanations (countering cherry-picking).
- **Strategy**: Before a major strategy / acquisition, run a red-team / blue-team debate; McKinsey and the military both use it to avoid groupthink, and research (Schwenk and others) shows devil's advocacy / dialectical inquiry outperforms simply seeking consensus.
- **2026**: An LLM is an extremely low-cost red team: it can instantly generate counterarguments, attack vectors, and edge cases. 2025 frameworks like BiasBuster even have LLMs debias one another. But an AI red team needs an explicit role assignment to overcome its agreeable tendency.
- Sources: https://www.cfr.org/books/red-team, https://lsaglobal.com/how-teams-avoid-groupthink/, https://www.mckinsey.com/industries/financial-services/our-insights/an-analytics-approach-to-debiasing-asset-management-decisions

### Reversibility Framework + Disagree and Commit (Bezos) · fit 5
*aka / source:* Type 1 / Type 2 decisions; one-way / two-way doors; high-velocity decision making (Amazon 2015–2016 shareholder letters)
- **What it is**: In his 2015–2016 shareholder letters, Bezos proposes that Type 1 decisions are irreversible (one-way doors) and demand careful deliberation, while Type 2 decisions are reversible (two-way doors) and should be made quickly by high-judgment individuals or small groups. He argues for deciding with about 70% of the information you wish you had (waiting for 90% is usually too slow) and being good at correcting course quickly. "Disagree and commit" lets a conviction-holder proceed without consensus: "I know we disagree on this, but will you gamble on it with me?"
- **Use in the process**: Classify the decision first: reversible? If reversible, decide fast and don't hold three meetings; only the irreversible ones get the heavyweight process (deliberation, consultation, pre-mortem). This counters decision paralysis (over-applying System 2 to a two-way door) and sunk cost (if reversible, try boldly and back out if wrong). When a disagreement won't resolve, use disagree-and-commit to place an explicit bet and execute fully, avoiding endless consensus-seeking.
- **Questions to ask**: "Is this a one-way or a two-way door? If it's a two-way door, why am I still hesitating?" "Am I waiting for 100% of the information — is 70% enough?" "Do we truly need consensus, or can we disagree and commit?" "If we're wrong, how costly is it to correct?"
- **Engineering**: A feature flag / gradually-rollable deployment turns a decision into a two-way door, so you can experiment fast; changing a DB schema, a public API contract, or deleting data is a one-way door needing heavyweight review. Architecturally, invest deliberately in reversibility (flags, blue-green, rollback-able migrations) to buy decision speed.
- **Product**: For reversible features (easy to retire, gradually rolled out), experiment fast; for irreversible ones (billing logic, the data model, external commitments), go slow. Use disagree-and-commit to break a PM / engineering deadlock over a feature.
- **Ops**: Run reversible operational experiments (copy, push notifications) fast and often; be careful with irreversible ones (data deletion, metric-definition changes that affect historical comparisons). Use the 70%-information principle to avoid analysis paralysis.
- **Strategy**: Allocate resources and review intensity to strategic bets by reversibility; the Amazon Studios "I disagree but will fully support it" example shows how a leader can place a bet healthily without consensus.
- **2026**: In an AI-accelerated environment, "decide at 70% information plus correct course quickly" has even more of an edge, because iteration and rollback costs fall; pair it with good reversibility engineering (flags, version control). Caution: AI making output faster does not mean treating a one-way door carelessly as a two-way door.
- Sources: https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2016-letter-to-shareholders, https://s2.q4cdn.com/299287126/files/doc_financials/annual/2015-Letter-to-Shareholders.PDF, https://www.realtimeperformance.com/what-we-can-learn-from-jeff-bezos-about-decision-making/

### Framing Effect（框架效應） · fit 5
*aka / source:* Valence framing; gain/loss framing; the Asian disease problem (Tversky & Kahneman 1981, *The Framing of Decisions and the Psychology of Choice*, Science 211:453-458)
- **What it is**: Logically and statistically equivalent options trigger different — even opposite — choices depending only on whether they are described as a gain or a loss. In the Asian disease problem, "200 of 600 saved" and "400 of 600 die" describe the identical outcome, yet a gain (survival) frame pushes people toward risk aversion (take the sure option) while a loss (death) frame pushes them toward risk seeking (take the gamble) — a preference reversal. This violates the *description-invariance* axiom of expected utility theory, and its mechanism is prospect theory's reference point plus loss aversion (the value function is concave for gains, convex for losses). Note this is *valence* framing — the *same* option reworded — and is distinct from broad/narrow framing, which is about widening the *set* of options considered.
- **Use in the process**: When evaluating any option, deliberately restate it in both the gain frame and the loss frame and check whether your preference holds; if it flips, you've found a framing artifact rather than a real preference. Strip emotionally loaded valence wording from option descriptions before deciding, and present quantities in a neutral, two-sided form (both the survival and the mortality number).
- **Questions to ask**: "If I re-described this option as a loss instead of a gain (or vice versa), would I still prefer it?" "Is the choice being driven by the substance of the outcomes or by the wording of the description?" "Whose reference point is this framed against, and who chose that reference point?"
- **Engineering**: Reframe metrics so reviewers don't get whipsawed: "99.9% uptime" (gain) vs "8.7 hours of downtime per year" (loss) are identical but pull risk appetite in opposite directions, so SLO / incident-budget discussions should show both. In experiment instrumentation, naming a metric `success_rate` vs `failure_rate` (or `churn` vs `retention`) will bias how the team reads the same dashboard — define and surface both poles. For tenant-isolation or data-loss risk, frame in the loss direction deliberately so the severity isn't softened away.
- **Product**: Pricing copy is pure framing: annual billing as "save 20%" (gain frame) converts very differently from monthly as "pay 25% more" (loss frame) for the identical price gap — A/B which frame to use, but know you are exploiting a framing effect, not a value difference. Feature messaging "unlock X" vs "don't lose access to X" likewise flips perceived value via loss aversion.
- **Ops**: When reporting a result, presenting a treatment as "survival rate" vs "mortality rate" can reverse a stakeholder's call on the same data — agree on a neutral framing convention before the readout, and report both the positive and negative pole of every rate. Watch for analysts (or yourself) unintentionally choosing the frame that flatters the desired conclusion.
- **Strategy**: In negotiation and stakeholder pitches, whoever sets the frame ("this is a cost-saving" vs "this is a revenue risk") tilts the risk appetite of the room; prepare your own framing of a strategic bet and, defensively, see through the counterparty's frame by re-deriving the underlying numbers frame-free.
- **2026**: 2025 research confirms LLMs are susceptible to framing: Germani & Spitale (2025, *Science Advances*) show that *source* framing — attributing an otherwise-identical statement to a different author (e.g. a Chinese vs. unnamed source) — systematically shifts model judgments, and 2024-2025 LLM-bias work likewise finds models respond differently to logically equivalent gain- vs loss-framed (valence) prompts. When using an LLM for evaluation or recommendations, present the same case in both frames (and strip source attribution) and compare, or normalize the wording, rather than trusting a single-framed answer.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Framing_effect_(psychology), https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.7455683, https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adz2924

### Survivorship Bias · fit 4
*aka / source:* the classic case of Abraham Wald's WWII bomber bullet-hole analysis
- **What it is**: Analyzing only the survivors / successes while ignoring the invisible casualties / failures, leading to a systematic misjudgment of cause. The classic: Wald argued that the parts to reinforce were where the returning bombers had no bullet holes (planes hit there never came back).
- **Use in the process**: Deliberately go find "the people not in your sample": churned customers, those who abandoned signup, the tests that failed. When analyzing successes, ask how many did the same thing and still failed. Collect failure / exit data rather than looking only at who stayed.
- **Questions to ask**: "Who can't I see — who has left, never entered, never responded?" "The thing the successes did — did the failures do it too?" "Is this conclusion built on a surviving sample?"
- **Engineering**: Inferring "the architecture is good" because "the services still running are all stable," ignoring why the services already retired died; in error tracking, looking only at reported errors while silent failures (client crashes, timeouts that never get sent) stay invisible.
- **Product**: Designing features by studying only active power users (survivors), ignoring the people who churned on day one of onboarding — yet the latter represent the bulk of the market.
- **Ops**: Celebrating only the winning A/B tests and not mining the learning from the losing ones; in retention analysis, looking only at the surviving cohort and ignoring the characteristics of those already churned (creating the illusion that "all our users are happy"). In multi-tenant analysis, looking only at active merchants overstates product health.
- **Strategy**: Using a successful unicorn's strategy as a template ("they all did this"), ignoring the many companies that died using the same strategy; competitor analysis also looks only at the surviving competitors.
- **2026**: In data pipelines, survivorship bias is often amplified by automation: modeling only users with a "completion event" (the training data naturally excludes those who left midway), so the AI model learns biased conclusions. Actively include censored / churned samples.
- Sources: https://medium.com/@falkgottlob/how-to-avoid-survivorship-bias-in-product-management-lessons-from-the-british-bomber-study-25edb8ab4ab7, https://www.analytics-toolkit.com/glossary/survivorship-bias/, https://www.causalityengine.ai/glossary/survivorship-bias

### Base-Rate Neglect · fit 4
*aka / source:* Base-rate fallacy; often produced by the representativeness heuristic
- **What it is**: Ignoring the overall statistical base rate and over-relying on specific, vivid case information. Kahneman & Tversky's "lawyer vs. engineer" experiment showed that once given a personality description, subjects ignored the true proportions of the two professions in the population. It is directly tied to Bayesian inference.
- **Use in the process**: Before any "this case is special" judgment, first ask what the base rate of that class of events is. Treat case-specific information as an adjustment to the base rate, not a replacement for it. Pay special attention to rare events (a rare bug, rare fraud): even a very accurate detector will produce more false alarms than true positives when the base rate is low.
- **Questions to ask**: "What's the overall incidence of this kind of thing?" "Am I being dragged along by one vivid case, forgetting it's actually rare?" "The detection accuracy is high, but at this low base rate, how many of the positives are real?"
- **Engineering**: Alerting / anomaly detection: a model is 99% accurate, but if the true anomaly rate is only 0.1%, the bulk of alerts are still false (the mathematical root of alert fatigue). Threshold design for security scanning and fraud detection must account for the base rate in a Bayesian way.
- **Product**: A large customer loudly demanding a feature (a vivid case) doesn't mean most tenants need it; look at the request's true proportion across the whole user base before prioritizing.
- **Ops**: Seeing a segment with high conversion and going all-in, while ignoring that the segment is only 2% of traffic (low base rate); when reading a funnel, combine rates with absolute magnitudes rather than looking at rates alone.
- **Strategy**: "Some competitor went viral on feature X" is one case; the overall success rate (base rate) of that class of bet is what strategy should weigh.
- **2026**: LLMs frequently re-enact base-rate neglect in probabilistic reasoning, getting pulled off by vivid descriptions; for high-stakes judgments (risk control, credit), don't take an LLM's intuitive probability as a number — apply a Bayesian correction with the actual base rate.
- Sources: https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/base-rate-fallacy, https://www.simplypsychology.org/base-rate-fallacy.html, https://www.cogn-iq.org/learn/theory/base-rate-neglect/

### Availability Heuristic · fit 4
*aka / source:* Availability bias (Tversky & Kahneman 1973)
- **What it is**: Judging an event's probability by how easily examples come to mind. Recent, emotionally charged, heavily reported events get their probability overstated, while the silent normal case is understated.
- **Use in the process**: Base major judgments on data frequencies, not the most vivid event in your head. Ask yourself: does this really happen often, or did it just happen recently / leave a strong impression? Replace "I remember last time…" with incident / event statistics.
- **Questions to ask**: "Do I think this is common because the data supports it, or because it just happened to me?" "Is there a more frequent but less salient problem I'm ignoring?"
- **Engineering**: Burned by a race condition last week, so this week's review over-fixates on concurrency while ignoring the statistically more common N+1 query / input validation. After an incident, over-hardening against "last time's" scenario (fighting the last war).
- **Product**: One customer complaint went viral in Slack this week, so the team prioritizes fixing it, but it may affect only a few people; return to ticket frequency / blast-radius data to prioritize.
- **Ops**: When interpreting metrics, letting "the recent big news event" dominate attribution while ignoring the long-term trend. The most prominent chart on a dashboard gets over-interpreted (salience = availability).
- **Strategy**: Overreacting and adjusting strategy because of one recent event (a competitor's funding round, one big churn); lengthen the time window and look at the base rate.
- **2026**: AI-generated content makes certain topics "seem to be everywhere," amplifying the availability bias — the common answer an LLM outputs makes you think it's the mainstream practice. Check it against actual adoption-rate / search-volume data.
- Sources: https://www.suebehaviouraldesign.com/en/blog/kahneman-thinking-fast-and-slow/, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thinking,_Fast_and_Slow, https://thedecisionlab.com/reference-guide/philosophy/system-1-and-system-2-thinking

### Hindsight Bias · fit 4
*aka / source:* I-knew-it-all-along effect; creeping determinism
- **What it is**: After an event, believing it was "predictable / obvious all along," which distorts our memory of how good the original judgment was. It feeds overconfidence and corrupts postmortems ("we should have known").
- **Use in the process**: At the moment of deciding, write down your assumptions, probabilities, and available information (a decision journal), and later judge the decision against that contemporaneous record rather than after-the-fact memory. Focus postmortems on "what information was obtainable at the time" rather than outcome-based hindsight.
- **Questions to ask**: "Before we knew the outcome, was this really that obvious?" "Am I evaluating the decision quality at the time, or has the outcome contaminated it?"
- **Engineering**: The biggest enemy of incident postmortems: feeling afterward that "this bug was so obvious, how did we miss it," which produces a blame culture instead of systemic improvement. Blameless postmortems and preserving the context at the time (arXiv 1707.03869 notes that lacking a historical record worsens hindsight bias) are the remedy.
- **Product**: After a feature fails, the team collectively claims "we always thought it wouldn't work," burying the real learning; compare against the predictions written down before launch.
- **Ops**: Looking back at a growth or decline and concocting a clean causal story after the fact, becoming overconfident in the attribution; acknowledge the uncertainty that existed at the time.
- **Strategy**: When reviewing a failed strategic bet, use the decision journal to separate "a bad decision" from "bad luck" (a good process can still produce a bad outcome).
- **2026**: Research (CogSci 2020) finds hindsight bias directly feeds overconfidence. The decision-journal concept has been integrated into AI tools as of 2025: use an LLM to structure the record of assumptions at decision time and auto-compare afterward, reducing memory contamination.
- Sources: https://cognitivesciencesociety.org/cogsci20/papers/0145/0145.pdf, https://www.scribbr.com/research-bias/overconfidence-bias/, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.03869

### Checklists · fit 4
*aka / source:* The Checklist Manifesto (Atul Gawande 2009); WHO Surgical Safety Checklist
- **What it is**: Using a simple list to counter the unreliability of memory and attention. Gawande distinguishes errors of ignorance (not knowing) from errors of ineptitude (knowing but failing to apply it); the checklist targets the latter, ensuring that the critical-but-mundane steps aren't missed in complex situations and forcing communication and ownership.
- **Use in the process**: Turn the decision steps that are repetitive and costly-to-miss into a checklist (DO-CONFIRM or READ-DO). Keep it lean (only the critical killer items) and run it at a natural pause point. Include the items that require communication / coordination too (who owns it, who confirms it).
- **Questions to ask**: "Which critical-but-boring steps tend to get skipped under pressure?" "Is this list short enough that it will actually be used?" "Which item, if missed, has the most severe consequence?"
- **Engineering**: Deploy / release checklist, PR-review checklist, incident-response runbook, safe-release checks (is the migration rollback-able, is the feature flag ready, is multi-tenant data verified). It is one of the most effective tools for institutionalizing senior experience and resisting overconfident "no need to check this time."
- **Product**: A new-feature GA checklist: is instrumentation ready, are permission / plan gating correct, i18n copy, a fallback plan, customer-support notification. A launch-readiness review is essentially a checklist.
- **Ops**: A pre-delivery analysis checklist: data source / time zone / deduplication, sample size, whether confounders are controlled, whether survivorship bias was checked, whether the hypothesis was pre-registered.
- **Strategy**: A major-decision checklist (like the pre-decision checks Kahneman discusses in Thinking, Fast and Slow) — did we do the outside view? Did we do a pre-mortem? Have we heard the opposing view?
- **2026**: In the AI era the checklist gains "AI-check items": has the AI-generated code / analysis been human-verified, has it been checked for hallucinations and sources. arXiv 2601.08045 explicitly recommends tooled checklists — TDD, CI/CD, code review — to counter biases under AI assistance.
- Sources: https://fs.blog/the-checklist-manifesto/, https://www.nateliason.com/notes/checklist-manifesto-atul-gawande, https://arxiv.org/html/2601.08045v1

### Nudge / Choice Architecture · fit 4
*aka / source:* Nudge (Thaler & Sunstein 2008); libertarian paternalism; the default effect
- **What it is**: Steering a decision by designing the context in which options are presented (the choice architecture), without restricting options or changing incentives. Core levers: defaults, framing, social proof, salience, and commitment devices. People tend to accept the default, so default design is enormously influential.
- **Use in the process**: Two dimensions: (a) outward — design the product's / process's choice architecture to steer users toward choices good for both them and the business; (b) inward — design the team's decision environment to debias (e.g. making "independent, simultaneous estimate reveal" the default process, making the pre-mortem a default kickoff step). Keep it ethical and avoid dark patterns.
- **Questions to ask**: "What is the default? Which way does it push people, and is that fair to the user?" "Can I change the context so the right choice becomes the path of least effort?" "Is this a nudge or manipulation (a dark pattern)?"
- **Engineering**: Set the safe / correct option as the default: safe configuration values, 2FA enabled by default, a second confirmation for dangerous operations (productive friction). In internal tooling, nudge toward good practices (PR template on by default, tests required by default).
- **Product**: Use nudges in onboarding: pre-select the recommended plan, use a progress bar (completion) and social proof ("most merchants have already enabled X") to lift activation; the default determines most users' final settings. In multi-tenant SaaS, the default plan configuration heavily influences adoption.
- **Ops**: Use experiments to measure the causal effect of different nudges (default, copy framing, social proof) on conversion / retention, not just correlation; mind ethics and long-term trust.
- **Strategy**: The choice architecture of a pricing page (anchoring on a high-tier plan, highlighting the recommended plan) directly drives ARPU; but strategically balance short-term conversion against long-term trust, avoiding a dark-pattern backlash.
- **2026**: Since 2020, "digital nudging / the recommender system as nudge" has matured, and 2024–2025 has brought "AI nudging" and its ethical-audit frameworks (arXiv 2304.14338); regulation (GDPR, dark-pattern rules) is tightening, so choice architecture needs to be auditable and free of manipulation.
- Sources: https://www.alexmurrell.co.uk/summaries/cass-sunstein-and-richard-thaler-nudge, https://yukaichou.com/behavioral-analysis/nudge-theory-thaler-sunstein-choice-architecture/, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.03413

### Status Quo Bias（現狀偏誤） · fit 4
*aka / source:* Default bias / inertia (Samuelson & Zeckhauser 1988, *Status Quo Bias in Decision Making*, Journal of Risk and Uncertainty 1:7-59)
- **What it is**: A disproportionate preference for maintaining the current state — or simply doing nothing — even when an objectively superior alternative is available; Samuelson and Zeckhauser demonstrated it empirically with faculty health-plan and retirement-fund choices. Its roots are loss aversion and the endowment effect (giving up the current option *feels* like a loss, weighted more heavily than the equivalent gain) plus regret avoidance / omission bias (people anticipate more regret from a bad outcome caused by *action* than from the same outcome reached by *inaction*). Because of this, whatever is set as the default inherits enormous inertia — the default effect (cf. Johnson & Goldstein 2003 on opt-in vs opt-out organ donation).
- **Use in the process**: Promote "keep the status quo" from a free, silently-accepted baseline to an explicit option that must be actively justified on the same terms as every alternative. Use zero-base re-selection: pretend nothing is chosen yet and pick from scratch given today's information. Institutionalize forced periodic re-choice (sunset/renewal reviews) so that *not deciding* stops being a way to default into continuation.
- **Questions to ask**: "If we weren't already doing this, would we choose to start it today?" "Are we keeping this because it's genuinely best, or because changing it requires effort and risks blame?" "What is the hidden cost of inaction here — what are we losing by *not* switching?"
- **Engineering**: Tech-stack and architecture inertia is textbook status quo bias — staying on a framework, queue, or search backend that the team agrees is worse, because migrating is effortful and a failed migration would draw blame (omission bias makes the failed action feel worse than the slow bleed of standing still). Counter it with zero-base architecture reviews that require the *current* stack to re-win on merits, and treat "do nothing" as a line item with its own carrying cost in the RFC.
- **Product**: Default plan, default settings, and pre-checked options dominate where most tenants end up, because users accept defaults rather than re-deciding — design defaults responsibly (this is the ethical edge of the default effect). Internally, a roadmap can suffer status quo bias too: under-investing in killing or reworking an entrenched feature because removing it is an active, blameable decision while leaving it is silent.
- **Ops**: Subscription renewal and auto-renew defaults run on status quo bias — quantify how much retention is genuine value versus inertia, since inertia-driven retention masks churn risk and inflates perceived product health. When a metric definition, dashboard default time range, or alert threshold was set long ago, force a periodic re-justification rather than letting it ride unexamined.
- **Strategy**: Resource allocation drifts toward last year's plan because re-allocating is an active, defensible-on-demand move while continuing is not; run zero-base budgeting and explicitly price the opportunity cost of strategic inaction. "Disagree and commit" and pre-committed kill criteria also help by making *changing course* a sanctioned, low-regret act.
- **2026**: 2024-2026 LLM cognitive-bias benchmarks find models also exhibit status-quo / default preference — they tend to favor the option presented as the incumbent or the first/default choice, which can quietly entrench an existing answer in agentic recommendation loops. When using an AI to evaluate "keep vs. change," reorder and relabel the options so the model isn't anchored on the status-quo framing.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Status_quo_bias, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00055564, https://rzeckhauser.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/status-quo-bias-decision-making
