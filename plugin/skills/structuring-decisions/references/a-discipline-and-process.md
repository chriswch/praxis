### Decision Intelligence (DI) · fit 5
*aka / source:* Decision Intelligence Engineering; coined and defined by Cassie Kozyrkov at Google
- **What it is**: Kozyrkov defines DI as "the discipline of turning information into better actions at any scale" — a new applied discipline that fuses applied data science, the social/behavioral sciences, and managerial science, and concerns itself with every facet of choosing between options. Its central inversion is to reason backwards from the decision you actually need to make to the data, tools, and process required, rather than treating analytics or ML as an end in itself.
- **Use in the process**: Write each consequential choice as a one-line "decision statement" first — who is choosing what, toward which objective, among which options. Design the decision (objectives, options, default action, criteria) before going to the data, so the data cannot reverse-engineer the conclusion. Do not over-process small decisions (she stresses that picking a lunch sandwich should not get the same rigor as a go-to-market strategy).
- **Questions to ask**: Before analyzing or modeling, ask: "What decision is this? With no data at all, which option is my default? What evidence would have to appear before I'd change that default? How much analysis is this decision worth?" Shift the question from "what does the data say" to "what information do I need to make this particular choice better."
- **Engineering**: For technology selection (e.g. migrating from Resque to Sidekiq, or swapping an ORM), write the decision statement and success criteria first, then gather benchmarks — so engineers don't pick on gut feel and dress it up with numbers afterward. Commit thresholds like "p99 latency must drop by X to justify the migration" up front.
- **Product**: Before building a feature (e.g. auto-applying loyalty points on the checkout page), define the decision ("build or not / which variant"), the target metric (checkout conversion), and the acceptable floor first, then schedule the spike and user research — so the work is data-driven rather than data-inspired.
- **Ops**: Before building a dashboard or metric, ask "which decision does this metric support? Without it, how would I decide?" — avoiding piles of vanity metrics nobody acts on, and tracing every metric back to a concrete operational decision.
- **Strategy**: Frame "should we enter a new market / launch a new subscription plan" as a full decision design — objectives, options, default action, criteria, and a value-of-information assessment — so strategy discussions center on the choice rather than on reporting.
- **2026**: Through 2025–2026 DI moved from niche to mainstream: Gartner published its first "Decision Intelligence Platforms" Magic Quadrant (Feb 2026) and forecasts that by 2027 half of all business decisions will be augmented or automated by AI agents using DI. Kozyrkov's 2024–2025 Maven course "Decision-Making with ChatGPT (DecisionGPT)" applies the DI process to LLM-assisted decisions, framing the LLM as an amplifier while the human still owns the decision design.
- Sources: https://medium.com/data-science/introduction-to-decision-intelligence-5d147ddab767, https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/introduction-decision-intelligence-cassie-kozyrkov, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassie_Kozyrkov, https://maven.com/cassie-kozyrkov/decisiongpt

### Anatomy of a Decision · fit 5
*aka / source:* Components of a decision; objectives / options / information / preferences (payoffs) / uncertainty / outcomes
- **What it is**: Decompose a decision into analyzable parts — objectives/criteria, options, available information and priors, preferences and payoffs, uncertainty, and the eventual outcome. Kozyrkov stresses that "a decision is only made once an irrevocable allocation of resources takes place" — until then you have an opinion, not a decision.
- **Use in the process**: Fill in a decision table across the six parts: state objectives and success criteria, enumerate options (including "do nothing"), inventory existing information and priors, define the payoff of each outcome and the uncertainty you can tolerate, and finally track the outcome. Any blank cell marks where the decision is exposed.
- **Questions to ask**: Ask: "What exactly is my objective, and by what criteria do I measure it? Have I listed all options, including the status quo? What do I know versus assume? What is each outcome worth to me? Which uncertainties are most lethal?"
- **Engineering**: An Architecture Decision Record (ADR) is the anatomy made concrete: Context (information/priors), Options (candidates), Decision (chosen option + criteria), Consequences (payoffs/outcomes). Write the trade-offs of a multi-tenant data-isolation choice across these six parts.
- **Product**: Write the "decision section" of a PRD: objective (lift 30-day retention), options (three onboarding flows), information (current funnel data), payoffs (expected retention per variant), uncertainty (sample size), and an outcome-tracking plan.
- **Ops**: Restructure operational reports to be decision-first: open by stating the decision to be supported and its criteria, then present information and uncertainty — rather than burying a "recommendation" after a wall of charts.
- **Strategy**: Use the anatomy for a major investment (build vs. buy a DI platform): state the objective explicitly, list all options, inventory knowns/unknowns, and quantify the payoff and downside of each scenario, as the board's working brief.
- **2026**: Gartner's DI platforms treat "decision modeling" (explicitly modeling objectives, options, constraints, and outcomes) as a core capability — effectively tooling the anatomy of a decision. Through 2025–2026 the emphasis is on modeling these parts explicitly for AI-agent decisions too, so they can be monitored and audited.
- Sources: https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/introduction-decision-intelligence-cassie-kozyrkov, https://medium.com/data-science/introduction-to-decision-intelligence-5d147ddab767, https://www.gartner.com/en/documents/5599159

### Separating Decision Quality from Outcome (Resulting) · fit 5
*aka / source:* The golden rule of decision analysis (Kozyrkov); resulting / outcome bias — Annie Duke, *Thinking in Bets*
- **What it is**: Kozyrkov's golden rule: "evaluate the quality of a decision using only the information available to the decision-maker at the time the decision was made." Annie Duke calls equating outcome quality with decision quality "resulting" — a form of outcome bias: a good outcome can be luck, and a bad outcome can follow a good decision (life is more like poker than chess, where chance and hidden information separate the bet from the result).
- **Use in the process**: When reviewing, score "process quality" and "outcome" in separate columns: a good process with a bad outcome should not be punished, and a bad process with a good outcome should not be rewarded. Keep a decision journal of the information and reasoning at the time, and judge only against that to defeat hindsight bias.
- **Questions to ask**: Ask: "If I replayed this knowing only what I knew then, is it still a good decision? How much of this success/failure was luck versus judgment? Is what I'm learning a process lesson or just noise?"
- **Engineering**: Run incident reviews blameless and resulting-proof: a risky change that happened not to cause an incident (bad process, good outcome) still gets scrutinized, while a by-the-book deploy that still failed (good process, bad outcome) leads to process fixes, not individual blame.
- **Product**: Separate process from outcome when judging features: an A/B winner may just be seasonal luck, and a rigorous experiment that comes back non-significant doesn't mean the hypothesis or method was wrong. Keep a decision journal recording the assumptions and expectations held at launch.
- **Ops**: Build an "experiment quality" score (sample size, pre-registration, randomization) independent of whether the experiment "won," so teams don't only celebrate winners while ignoring process flaws and learning noise.
- **Strategy**: In strategy reviews (e.g. a past pricing change) distinguish "was the bet sound" from "how did the cards fall," so the organization doesn't institutionalize a fragile strategy off one lucky win.
- **2026**: Decision journaling and pre-registration spread through product and engineering teams across the 2020s; Duke's *Quit* (2022) extends the discipline to "when to walk away." This matters acutely in the AI era: trusting an LLM's advice because of one good result is resulting applied to an agent — it should be judged on process quality and calibration instead.
- Sources: https://kozyrkov.medium.com/are-you-a-bad-decision-maker-34690deae223, https://www.sachinrekhi.com/p/thinking-in-bets-annie-duke, https://calvinrosser.com/notes/thinking-in-bets-annie-duke/

### Decide How to Decide Before You See the Data · fit 5
*aka / source:* Default action + pre-set criteria; data-driven vs data-inspired (Kozyrkov)
- **What it is**: Kozyrkov argues you should do two things before looking at the data: (1) commit to a "default action" — which option is the lesser evil under ignorance; and (2) set the criteria/threshold required to overturn that default. She distinguishes data-driven (the data genuinely drives the decision) from data-inspired (browsing data until some signal catches your eye). Her example: being told a hotel scores 4.2 before being asked whether to book destroys your ability to be data-driven, because you'll now interpret 4.2 through your pre-existing lean.
- **Use in the process**: Fix the order: write "with no new data, I would choose X (default)" → then "I'll only switch to Z if I see Y (an explicit threshold/statistical criterion)" → then acquire the data → then act against the threshold. Looking at the numbers before setting the threshold is licensing yourself to rationalize after the fact.
- **Questions to ask**: Ask: "Before I see any data, what is my default choice? What explicit evidence (direction, magnitude, confidence level) would be enough to overturn it? Is this a threshold I'm willing to write down now and not quietly adjust later?"
- **Engineering**: Set the SLA threshold before a performance optimization or migration: "p99 must drop by ≥20% with no increase in error rate, or this PR doesn't merge," recorded in the PR description before load testing — so you don't cherry-pick a favorable reading from an ambiguous benchmark. Same for capacity planning: define the scale-out trigger first.
- **Product**: Pre-register the A/B test before launch: default to the status quo, and fix the primary metric, minimum detectable effect, and significance level up front; compare results only against those, foreclosing p-hacking and "let me slice one more segment."
- **Ops**: Give each operational KPI an "action threshold" rather than just watching the trend: e.g. "return rate >X% for two consecutive weeks triggers a supplier review," so data actually drives action instead of supplying a post-hoc story.
- **Strategy**: Before entering a new market, set "kill and double-down criteria": default to no investment, and require a specified leading indicator within 6 months before committing more budget — guarding against sunk-cost-driven expansion.
- **2026**: This is exactly the theory behind experimentation-platform pre-registration / guardrail metrics and feature-flag progressive rollout, standard in mature SaaS by 2025–2026. Setting criteria up front matters even more with LLM-assisted analysis, where it's easy to be led by model-generated "insights" — the data-inspired risk is amplified by AI.
- Sources: https://hbr.org/2019/06/the-first-thing-great-decision-makers-do, https://kozyrkov.medium.com/data-inspired-5c78db3999b2, https://www.success.com/cassie-kozyrkov-decision-intelligence

### One-Way vs Two-Way Door Decisions (Type 1 / Type 2) · fit 5
*aka / source:* Jeff Bezos Type 1 / Type 2 decisions; one-way door / two-way door; Amazon 1997 & 2015–2016 shareholder letters
- **What it is**: In Amazon's 2015 shareholder letter, Bezos distinguished consequential and (nearly) irreversible "one-way door" decisions (Type 1) — once through, you can't come back — which demand caution, deliberation, and broad consultation, from the majority of decisions, which are changeable and reversible "two-way doors" (Type 2) that "should be made quickly by high-judgment individuals or small groups." The 2016 letter adds that reversible decisions deserve a lightweight process and can be sped up with "disagree and commit." The earliest reflections trace to the 1997 letter.
- **Use in the process**: Classify before deciding: is this door one-way or two-way? Two-way → delegate, time-box, keep the process light, and iterate fast; one-way → escalate, slow down, run expected-value / decision-tree analysis, and require multiple viewpoints. Avoid both mismatches: treating a two-way door as one-way (over-analysis, organizational sclerosis = "Day 2") and treating a one-way door as two-way (recklessness causing irreversible harm).
- **Questions to ask**: Ask: "If this choice is wrong, how fast and how cheaply can I reverse it? Is the reversal cost on the order of minutes, a quarter, or irreversible? What level and weight of process matches its reversibility?"
- **Engineering**: Let reversibility set the deployment strategy: a feature flag or an experiment you can kill instantly is a two-way door — one person can merge and ramp it gradually; a one-shot destructive schema migration, a dropped column, or a public API contract change is a one-way door requiring review, backups, and staged rollout. Reversibility maps directly to rollback cost.
- **Product**: Route feature decisions by reversibility: reversible UI/copy/ordering ships fast in small steps; irreversible ones (data model, billing logic, externally committed SLAs) get careful design. Treat "ship a kill-switchable version first" as a way to turn a one-way door into a two-way door.
- **Ops**: Tag decisions with a "reversibility" label in the decision-tracking system, so the team concentrates analysis on the few one-way doors rather than running heavy analysis on every small choice.
- **Strategy**: Platform-level choices (build payments vs. integrate a third party, selecting a multi-tenant isolation model) are mostly one-way doors needing expected-value/scenario analysis and senior consensus; marketing campaigns and subscription-plan tweaks are mostly two-way doors to delegate and test fast.
- **2026**: Reversibility thinking fused deeply with trunk-based development, feature flags, and progressive delivery across the 2020s — engineering a one-way door into a two-way door (via canaries, shadow traffic, reversible migrations) is now a core competency. In the AI era, decisions handed to autonomous agents should be confined to reversible Type-2 territory, with one-way doors kept human-in-the-loop.

### The Three Pillars of Decision Intelligence (Data Science + Behavioral Science + Managerial Science) · fit 4
*aka / source:* The interdisciplinary pillars of Decision Intelligence; applied data science / behavioral (social) science / managerial science
- **What it is**: Kozyrkov describes DI as the fusion of three existing disciplines: applied data science (quantitative methods for extracting information and uncertainty from data), behavioral/social science (how people perceive, which biases they carry, how they set criteria and trade-offs), and managerial science (landing decisions in organizations, processes, incentives, and accountability). She frames DI engineering as "augmenting data science with the behavioral and managerial sciences."
- **Use in the process**: Check whether any decision covers all three pillars: is the quantitative side (data/models/statistics) handled well? Is the human side (biases, incentives, criteria) designed in? Is the organizational side (who decides, who executes, how it's monitored) explicit? Whichever pillar is missing is where the decision will fail.
- **Questions to ask**: Ask: "Is the bottleneck here insufficient data, human bias, or that no one owns it organizationally?" Many apparent data problems are really managerial or behavioral problems — picking the wrong pillar wastes resources in the wrong place.
- **Engineering**: In post-incident reviews, decompose by the three pillars: was the gap insufficient monitoring data (data science), the on-call engineer's judgment bias (behavioral), or undefined on-call ownership (managerial)? Avoid reflexively adding a dashboard every time without changing the process.
- **Product**: When a feature isn't adopted, diagnose by pillar: is it missing usage data, onboarding that induces cognitive load (behavioral), or unclear PM/engineering ownership so no one drives it (managerial)?
- **Ops**: Build a capability map for the analytics team: beyond pure SQL/BI (data science), is there someone who understands experimental-design bias (behavioral), and is there a mechanism to land and track decisions (managerial)? Shore up the weakest pillar.
- **Strategy**: When evaluating a "data-driven transformation" proposal, remind leadership that buying BI tools (data science) isn't enough — it also needs investment in decision literacy (behavioral) and decision-rights governance (managerial); ROI requires all three pillars.
- **2026**: Gartner's 2025–2026 definition of DI platforms echoes these three pillars: a platform must combine decision modeling (managerial science), analytics/AI (data science), and governance/monitoring, shifting the governance focus from "data governance" to "decision governance" (how decisions are designed, executed, monitored, and audited).
- Sources: https://medium.com/data-science/introduction-to-decision-intelligence-5d147ddab767, https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/introduction-decision-intelligence-cassie-kozyrkov, https://grounded-architecture.io/decision-intelligence, https://www.gartner.com/en/documents/5599159

### Normative / Descriptive / Prescriptive Decision Theory · fit 4
*aka / source:* Normative / Descriptive / Prescriptive decision theory
- **What it is**: The classic tripartite division of decision theory. Normative theory studies how a fully rational agent should decide under perfect information and utility maximization (e.g. expected-utility theory). Descriptive theory studies how people actually decide, including bounded rationality and irrationality (e.g. prospect theory). Prescriptive work applies the first two to real situations — "helping rational-but-imperfect people do better in practice" (e.g. the Analytic Hierarchy Process, AHP). The three map to: what one rationally should do, what people actually do, and how people can do better.
- **Use in the process**: For any decision, locate which layer you're in: are you computing the ideal optimum (normative), explaining/predicting human behavior (descriptive), or designing a process that makes real people less error-prone (prescriptive)? Organizational decisions should almost always land in the prescriptive layer — use the normative answer as a north star, use descriptive findings to predict deviation, then design a process that lands.
- **Questions to ask**: Ask: "What is the theoretical optimum (normative)? Where will real users/teams deviate (descriptive)? What guardrails and defaults should I design to close the gap (prescriptive)?"
- **Engineering**: Designing alert thresholds or autoscaling policy: normative gives the theoretically optimal threshold; descriptive warns that on-call engineers suffer alert fatigue and anchoring; prescriptive leads you to tiered alerts and a runbook so a real human gets it right at 3 a.m.
- **Product**: Pricing or plan design: normative computes the revenue-maximizing price; descriptive brings in prospect theory (loss aversion, anchoring) to predict user reactions; prescriptive uses that to design a decoy plan and default option.
- **Ops**: Reading A/B results: normative is the statistically optimal reading; descriptive reminds analysts of confirmation bias; prescriptive requires pre-registering hypotheses and criteria to constrain human bias.
- **Strategy**: Competitive game analysis: normative computes equilibria with game theory; descriptive concedes that both you and your rivals are less than fully rational; prescriptive chooses a robust rather than a fragile-optimal strategy.
- **2026**: Behavioral decision research across the 2020s keeps integrating the three perspectives (e.g. critical reviews such as the 2021 MDPI piece). In the AI era, normative frameworks are used to define an AI agent's "rationality baseline," descriptive research is used to correct systematic biases in LLMs and human users, and prescriptive design becomes the theoretical basis for human-in-the-loop and decision guardrails.
- Sources: https://www.mdpi.com/1911-8074/14/10/490, https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-63026-7_2, https://thedecisionlab.com/reference-guide/psychology/decision-theory, https://www.sas.upenn.edu/~baron/papers/normative.pdf

### Expected Value & Decision Trees · fit 4
*aka / source:* Expected Monetary Value (EMV); decision tree analysis; expected utility
- **What it is**: A quantitative method for choosing under uncertainty: expected value / expected monetary value, EMV = Σ(probability of each outcome × value of that outcome) − cost. A decision tree expands a multi-stage choice using "decision nodes" (squares, controllable options) and "chance nodes" (circles, uncertain outcomes), computes the EMV of each branch, and picks the path with the highest EMV (or, for risk-averse decision-makers, the highest expected utility).
- **Use in the process**: Draw the options and their uncertain outcomes as a tree, label each branch with its probability and payoff, roll back from the leaves to compute the EMV/expected utility of each option, then pick the highest. When sensitive to downside risk, switch to a utility function or also inspect the worst case rather than relying on expected value alone.
- **Questions to ask**: Ask: "For each option, what are the possible outcomes, their probabilities, and their value (money/utility)? After multiplying probability by payoff, which option has the highest expected value? Can I survive the worst case? Which probability/payoff estimate is most sensitive — and therefore worth gathering more information on?"
- **Engineering**: Decide whether to invest time fixing an intermittent bug: P(recurrence) × impact cost vs. the engineering hours to fix; or evaluate a test-automation investment: adoption cost vs. P(regression defect) × incident cost in expected savings — use EMV to set priority.
- **Product**: Feature go/no-go: draw a decision tree — invest $X to build, with success probability p yielding revenue V and failure probability (1−p) costing the investment; build if EMV > the do-nothing baseline (as in the worked example of a $2M investment with EMV $2.175M). Also useful for build vs. buy.
- **Ops**: Feed experiment results into a decision tree: use the experiment-estimated distribution of conversion-rate lift to compute the expected incremental revenue of a full rollout vs. its operating cost, supporting the roll-out decision.
- **Strategy**: Model staged strategic investment (pilot first, then full rollout) as a decision tree: only a successful pilot unlocks the next stage of investment; computing an EMV that captures this "option value" avoids going all-in at once.
- **2026**: 2025 tooling (Asana and others) turns decision trees + expected value into templated workflows; inside DI platforms, EMV/decision trees are the underlying logic of prescriptive analytics and automated agent decisions. Note: the calibration of the probability estimates matters more than the formula itself — LLM-generated probabilities are often poorly calibrated and must be validated against historical data.
- Sources: https://asana.com/resources/decision-tree-analysis, https://www.brainbok.com/guide/pm-study-notes/risk/expected-monetary-value-emv, https://www.open.edu/openlearn/money-business/decision-trees-and-dealing-uncertainty/content-section-4.1, https://www.pmi.org/learning/library/decision-tree-analysis-expected-utility-8214

### Behavioral / Descriptive Decision Making (Cognitive Biases & Prospect Theory) · fit 4
*aka / source:* Prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky); cognitive biases; bounded rationality (Herbert Simon)
- **What it is**: Descriptive theory explains how people actually decide — often departing from rationality. Prospect theory shows that the pain of a loss outweighs the pleasure of an equivalent gain (loss aversion), that people evaluate relative to a reference point rather than absolute levels, and that they distort small probabilities (overweighting rare events). Simon's bounded rationality holds that cognitive and informational limits lead people to "satisfice" rather than optimize. Common biases include anchoring, confirmation bias, hindsight bias, and the availability heuristic.
- **Use in the process**: When designing a decision process, anticipate and hedge biases: anchoring → estimate independently before discussing; confirmation bias → assign a devil's advocate or run a premortem; loss aversion → re-frame the options against a consistent reference point. Treat the systematic errors people make as a designable variable.
- **Questions to ask**: Ask: "What number are we anchored on right now? Am I seeking evidence to support a conclusion I already hold, or genuinely testing it? Would my preference flip if I re-framed these options against a different reference point? Which bias is most likely to bite us in this decision?"
- **Engineering**: Hedge optimism bias / the planning fallacy in estimation and capacity planning: use reference-class forecasting (look at how long similar past tasks actually took) rather than estimating from scratch; structure code review so reviewers read independently before discussing, so the first reviewer's opinion doesn't anchor the rest.
- **Product**: Use prospect theory in pricing and plans: present a struck-through original price + discount (reference point) and frame annual billing as "save X" rather than monthly as "pay Y more" to invoke loss aversion — but balance against ethics/compliance and avoid dark patterns.
- **Ops**: Guard against analyst bias: present the raw baseline before the conclusion to prevent anchoring; require replication of "significant" results to counter confirmation bias; watch for survivorship bias (looking only at retained-user data).
- **Strategy**: Hedge overconfidence and availability bias in competitor and market judgments: force a list of "where we could be wrong" plus base rates, and run a premortem imagining why the strategy failed.
- **2026**: Kahneman et al.'s *Noise* (2021) extends the focus from bias (systematic deviation) to "noise" (random scatter in judgments of the same kind), proposing "decision hygiene" and the mediating-assessments protocol. 2025–2026 research finds that LLMs exhibit human-like anchoring, framing, and other biases, and can amplify biases in their training data — making bias mitigation a central design concern in AI-assisted decisions.
- Sources: https://www.mdpi.com/1911-8074/14/10/490, https://thedecisionlab.com/reference-guide/psychology/decision-theory, https://www.academia.edu/12576145/Decision_Making_Descriptive_Normative_and_Prescriptive_Interactions

### Decision Rights: RAPID · fit 4
*aka / source:* Bain & Company RAPID; Recommend / Agree / Perform / Input / Decide; HBR 2006 *Who Has the D?*
- **What it is**: A decision-roles framework from Bain, popularized by the 2006 HBR article *Who Has the D?* (Rogers & Blenko). Five roles: Recommend (gathers input, generates options, and proposes a recommendation with rationale against the criteria), Agree (an explicit veto/sign-off, kept distinct from ordinary input), Perform (executes once decided), Input (supplies facts and insight but holds no sign-off), and Decide (the single final decision-maker who commits the organization to act). The aim is to make "who is responsible for what" transparent and eliminate committee-style gridlock.
- **Use in the process**: For any stuck or recurring decision, assign all five roles one by one (above all, ensure there is exactly one D); separate the person with a real veto (A) from those who merely give input (I), so it isn't the case that everyone feels entitled to block. Put the assignment in the header of the decision document.
- **Questions to ask**: Ask: "Who is the single Decide on this? Who holds a genuine veto (A) and who is only Input? Has the Recommend role received all the Input it should have? Which role is currently vacant or overlapping and causing the gridlock?"
- **Engineering**: Apply RAPID to a cross-team technical decision (a shared-platform API change): a platform staff engineer Recommends, security/SRE are Agree, consuming teams give Input, the engineering director Decides, the implementing team Performs — so the RFC doesn't get discussed endlessly in Slack with nobody calling it.
- **Product**: Feature prioritization and roadmap trade-offs: the PM is Decide, design and engineering are Input, legal/compliance may be Agree, related teams Perform — so things still converge even with many stakeholders.
- **Ops**: Establish decision rights for "metric definition changes": the data team Recommends, finance/ops are Agree (because it affects reporting), and a single data owner Decides — so the same metric doesn't fragment into multiple definitions.
- **Strategy**: Governing customization requests in a multi-tenant SaaS: sales gives Input, product Recommends, the CTO/CEO Decides whether it goes into the mainline, engineering Performs — preventing a big customer's demand from bypassing the process and hijacking the roadmap.
- **2026**: In the remote/matrixed organizations of the 2020s, RAPID is used alongside DACI and RACI to counter decision delay; Gartner's 2025–2026 "decision governance" discourse treats clarifying decision rights as a prerequisite for landing DI platforms, extending it to a new question of "which decisions can be delegated to AI agents and which keep a human Decide."
- Sources: https://www.bain.com/insights/rapid-decision-making/, https://www.toolshero.com/decision-making/rapid-decision-making-model/, https://www.theuncertaintyproject.org/tools/rapid-framework

### Prioritizing Decisions & Value of Information · fit 4
*aka / source:* Does this decision deserve effort?; value of information (VoI); analysis proportional to stakes (Kozyrkov)
- **What it is**: A meta-principle of DI: not every decision deserves equal analysis. Kozyrkov stresses prioritizing first — "what goes on your lunch sandwich shouldn't get the same rigor as a new product's GTM strategy." The companion concept is value of information (VoI): gather information only when acquiring it might change the decision and the value of that change exceeds the cost of acquiring it. Sometimes analysis reveals that "no fact would change your choice," in which case you should stop analyzing and act.
- **Use in the process**: Triage each decision with three questions: how big is the bet? How reversible is it (Type-1/2)? Could information change the choice? Allocate analysis budget accordingly. If you find that no obtainable information would change the default action, stop analyzing immediately, execute, and move your effort elsewhere.
- **Questions to ask**: Ask: "How much time is this decision worth? If I obtained this data, would it actually change my choice? Does the value of the change exceed the cost of gathering it? Have I in fact already decided and am just stalling?"
- **Engineering**: Counter analysis paralysis and over-engineering: a small reversible internal-tooling choice doesn't need a three-day spike; an irreversible data-model decision warrants a full POC. Use VoI to decide whether to write one more benchmark.
- **Product**: Allocate research budget: ship low-risk reversible UI changes and observe; reserve expensive user research and large-sample experiments for high-stakes, irreversible features — instead of queuing research for every small decision.
- **Ops**: Prioritize ad-hoc analysis requests: ask first "which decision will this analysis change, and what is that decision worth," decline requests that won't change any action, and invest analytical capacity in high-VoI questions.
- **Strategy**: Match research depth to the stakes of the bet: a small pilot launched quickly replaces lengthy market research; only platform-level, irreversible investments warrant consultant-grade due diligence.
- **2026**: VoI is a classic concept from Bayesian decision theory; DI popularizes it into a daily working habit. Through 2025–2026, with LLM assistance, the marginal cost of gathering and synthesizing information has collapsed — which paradoxically makes the meta-judgment of "is this decision worth making, and when to stop gathering" more important, along with resisting the urge to "keep analyzing because it's now so easy to."
- Sources: https://medium.com/data-science/introduction-to-decision-intelligence-5d147ddab767, https://hbr.org/2019/06/the-first-thing-great-decision-makers-do, https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/introduction-decision-intelligence-cassie-kozyrkov

### OODA Loop (Observe–Orient–Decide–Act) · fit 3
*aka / source:* Boyd's decision cycle; Observe–Orient–Decide–Act
- **What it is**: A decision cycle developed by US Air Force Colonel John Boyd in the 1970s: Observe (gather multi-source data in real time) → Orient (combine mental models, experience, and context into understanding) → Decide (choose an action based on that orientation) → Act (execute, with feedback flowing into the next loop). The core insight: winning isn't about absolute speed but about completing the loop faster than the environment changes — and Orient is the most critical step, since a fast loop oriented wrong loses to a slow loop oriented right.
- **Use in the process**: In fast-changing or adversarial situations, deliberately shorten the loop: build real-time observation (monitoring), invest in Orient (update mental models and assumptions rather than reusing a stale frame), minimize friction between Decide and Act, and use each round's result to correct the next round's orientation.
- **Questions to ask**: Ask: "Is my observation real-time enough? Is my mental model (orientation) still correct, or am I running on an outdated worldview? How long is my observe-to-act loop, and how fast is the environment changing? How does last round's result correct this round's orientation?"
- **Engineering**: Incident response and on-call are OODA: Observe (monitoring/alerts/dashboards) → Orient (locate the failure domain) → Decide (roll back or patch) → Act, with MTTR measuring loop speed; chaos engineering is peacetime training for a better Orient.
- **Product**: Agile build-measure-learn is essentially OODA: the point isn't just shipping fast but Orient — whether you can correctly update your understanding of demand from user behavior, so you don't quickly build the wrong thing.
- **Ops**: Build a fast loop for real-time operational monitoring (e.g. conversion, inventory, and payment success during a sales peak): dashboard (Observe) → analyst reading (Orient) → trigger an operational decision (Decide/Act), shortening data latency to speed the loop.
- **Strategy**: When a competitor moves fast (a price cut or a new feature), use OODA to orient and respond faster than they do — emphasizing that strategic agility isn't reckless charging but better orientation.
- **2026**: OODA was widely borrowed across the 2020s into cybersecurity, SRE, and competitive strategy; in the AI era, automated systems (autoscaling, anomaly detection, AI agents) are compressing the Observe→Act loop to sub-second, pushing the human role up to Orient (designing mental models/goals) and to supervising one-way-door decisions.
