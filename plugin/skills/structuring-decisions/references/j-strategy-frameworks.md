### Wardley Mapping · fit 5
*aka / source:* Wardley Maps; Value Chain Mapping; by Simon Wardley
- **What it is**: A visual strategy tool created by Simon Wardley. The vertical axis is the value chain (anchored at a user need at the top and descending to underlying components, representing visibility to the user); the horizontal axis is the evolution stage: Genesis (novel) → Custom-Built → Product/Rental → Commodity/Utility. Placing each component by its evolutionary position produces a map of the whole landscape, so you can reason about how the terrain will move rather than just listing components.
- **Use in the process**: Draw the map first to build shared situational awareness: place each capability/technical component at its evolutionary stage, then ask of each, "should we build it, buy a product, or rent a commodity?" Components near commodity get outsourced or run on managed cloud services; only Genesis/Custom components warrant differentiating in-house R&D. The map also turns build-vs-buy and technology selection from a clash of intuitions into a shared, debatable picture of the terrain.
- **Questions to ask**: What evolutionary stage has this component reached? What is the customer's true anchor need, and are we putting resources at the right point in the value chain? What are we still building in-house that has already commoditized, where we should stop investing? Could a competitor exploit a component's evolution (e.g. AI) to commoditize our value proposition?
- **Engineering**: The strongest framework for technology selection and build-vs-buy: map components like search (Elasticsearch/Chewy), queues (Sidekiq/Resque), payment gateway, shipping, CDN; run the commodity ones (basic compute, transactional email) on managed services and build only the Genesis/Custom ones (e.g. multi-tenant checkout-optimization logic). It also diagnoses tech debt: still hand-building a long-commoditized component is wasted effort.
- **Product**: When planning the roadmap, identify which features are sliding from Product toward Commodity (e.g. basic payment integrations), pull engineering off those table-stakes capabilities, and reinvest in still-Genesis/Custom differentiators (e.g. AI merchandising, intelligent fulfillment matching).
- **Ops**: Annotate each component's maturity on the map and cross-reference it with operational cost and failure rate to surface "high-maintenance yet already-commoditized" components as priority candidates for replacement or outsourcing.
- **Strategy**: A core platform-strategy tool: plan gameplays that deliberately commoditize a competitor's value proposition (e.g. turning a paid add-on into a built-in free feature), or anticipate how AI will reshape the e-commerce technology stack and stake out the Genesis components early.
- **2026**: Through 2025–2026, AI is the single biggest accelerant of commoditization: capabilities that were Custom/Product (search, recommendations, support, copywriting) are being pushed rapidly toward commodity by LLMs. A Wardley lens helps you judge which in-house capabilities are about to lose their differentiation, which is why it is currently one of the strategy-mapping methods most championed by engineering leaders.
- Sources: https://www.wardleymaps.com/guides/wardley-mapping-101, https://www.wardleymaps.com/glossary/evolution-stages, https://www.bmc.com/blogs/wardley-value-chain-mapping/, https://beyondthebacklog.com/2025/01/14/wardley-maps/

### Strategic Choice Cascade (Playing to Win) · fit 5
*aka / source:* Playing to Win; Strategic Choice Cascade; by A.G. Lafley & Roger Martin (2013)
- **What it is**: Five mutually reinforcing strategic choices from Roger Martin and former P&G CEO A.G. Lafley: (1) winning aspiration, (2) where to play, (3) how to win, (4) what capabilities must be in place, (5) what management systems are required. The heart of the cascade is where-to-play plus how-to-win, and the five choices must be internally consistent — you toggle back and forth between them until they cohere.
- **Use in the process**: For major directional decisions, fill in all five boxes and test for consistency: does the chosen where-to-play (target market/segment/channel) genuinely fit the chosen how-to-win (basis of differentiation)? Strategy is choice — explicitly write down which attractive opportunities you are saying no to. Any change to one box sends you back to re-check the other four.
- **Questions to ask**: Do our where-to-play and how-to-win actually reinforce each other, or are they disconnected? What are we giving up in order to win on this battlefield? Do we have the capabilities required to win, or are we fighting where we have no advantage?
- **Engineering**: Translate "how to win = technical differentiation" into capability building: if how-to-win is "the fastest, most reliable checkout," engineering capability should go into checkout latency and fault-tolerant payment retries (mirroring the payment-retry example in the commit-convention reference); avoid over-engineering on battlefields that don't matter.
- **Product**: A gate for new-feature and new-module decisions: does this feature serve our chosen where-to-play segment? Does it strengthen how-to-win, or is it just "competitors have it so we should too"? Helps product teams say no to tempting but off-strategy requests.
- **Ops**: The fifth box, management systems, maps directly to the metrics you track: confirm the dashboards measure leading indicators of how-to-win rather than vanity metrics. Use data to validate the real profitability and retention of the where-to-play segment.
- **Strategy**: A sharp tool for market-segmentation decisions in multi-tenant SaaS: where-to-play can be specified down to "mid-market retail brands vs micro-merchants vs cross-border sellers," and how-to-win then determines whether you win on ecosystem breadth, price, or vertical depth — forcing the team to make trade-offs rather than try to serve everyone.
- **2026**: Through 2023–2025 Roger Martin has continued writing on Medium that where-to-play and how-to-win are inseparable, and has criticized the AI-era habit of treating "use AI" as a strategy while ignoring that it must serve an explicit winning logic.
- Sources: https://fs.blog/playing-to-win-how-strategy-really-works/, https://rogermartin.medium.com/decoding-the-strategy-choice-cascade-475d40555eb1, https://rogermartin.medium.com/on-the-inseparability-of-where-to-play-and-how-to-win-181c2ea5c463, https://mooncamp.com/blog/playing-to-win-book-summary

### The Kernel (Good Strategy / Bad Strategy) · fit 5
*aka / source:* The Kernel; by Richard Rumelt (2011)
- **What it is**: Richard Rumelt argues that good strategy has a logical structure he calls the kernel, with three parts: a diagnosis (an interpretation that identifies the critical challenge and the crux obstacle), a guiding policy (the overall approach for dealing with the challenge — like guardrails, it channels and constrains action without specifying every step), and a set of coherent actions (coordinated steps and resource commitments that reinforce rather than fight one another). The most common root cause of bad strategy is a weak or absent diagnosis; a close second is mistaking goals for strategy.
- **Use in the process**: Before drafting any strategy document, force the diagnosis first: what is the actual problem we must solve? Then write a single guiding policy, and finally list mutually reinforcing actions that don't work against each other. If the document is just a pile of goals and slogans (grow 30%, be number one) with no diagnosis, it is bad strategy in Rumelt's sense.
- **Questions to ask**: What is our real challenge — not just the result we want? Where is the crux obstacle? Is this plan dressing up a vision or a goal as a strategy? Are these actions coherent and concentrated, or a scattered wish list?
- **Engineering**: Use it for technical-refactor and architecture proposals: start with a diagnosis (e.g. "checkout-service P99 latency is high and is bleeding conversion"), give a guiding policy ("make synchronous calls asynchronous; remove dependencies from the hot path"), then list coherent actions. Avoid proposals that just state "make it faster and more reliable" with no diagnosis.
- **Product**: Strips "pseudo-strategy" out of quarterly product planning: push goals like "raise NPS" or "grow MAU" back down to the diagnosis layer — why is retention poor? Is the crux onboarding, or is the core value not landing? The guiding policy follows from the diagnosis.
- **Ops**: The diagnosis stage is where data analysis earns its keep: use funnels, cohorts, and root-cause analysis to locate the crux obstacle so the guiding policy rests on evidence rather than intuition. Rumelt stresses that a weak diagnosis is the leading cause of bad strategy — and weak diagnoses usually come from not looking at the data.
- **Strategy**: A checklist for leadership strategy reviews: decompose the company's strategy into the kernel's three parts; if any part is missing or is just a slogan, send it back to be redone. For SaaS especially, it punctures the common failure of treating a goal as a strategy ("our strategy is to use AI / to IPO / to go international").
- **2026**: In a 2024 Lenny's Podcast interview, Rumelt suggested replacing the overused word "strategy" with "action agenda" to emphasize executability. The Wardley Mapping community often uses a Wardley map to generate the diagnosis and then feeds it into Rumelt's guiding policy.
- Sources: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/good-strategy-bad-strategy-richard, https://www.alexmurrell.co.uk/summaries/richard-rumelt-good-strategy-bad-strategy, https://learnwardleymapping.com/2022/03/27/rumelts-good-strategy-bad-strategy-and-wardley-mapping/

### 7 Powers · fit 5
*aka / source:* Seven Powers; by Hamilton Helmer (2016)
- **What it is**: Hamilton Helmer identifies seven Powers that produce persistent differential returns: Scale Economies, Network Economies, Counter-Positioning, Switching Costs, Branding, Cornered Resource, and Process Power. Each Power must combine a Benefit (higher price or lower cost) with a Barrier (something that prevents competitors from arbitraging that benefit away). Power is about persistent differential returns — it is not growth, not "operating well," and not a temporary lead.
- **Use in the process**: For long-horizon investment, M&A, or platform decisions, ask "which Power does this build or strengthen, and what is its Barrier?" An advantage with no Barrier will eventually be copied and isn't worth treating as a strategic pivot. Helmer stresses that strategy is building and holding at least one Power; everything else is in support of it.
- **Questions to ask**: Which genuine Power do we have, versus a merely temporary lead? If a competitor tried to copy us, what Barrier would they face (and if there is none, the advantage isn't durable)? Is there a counter-positioning opportunity — a move the incumbent won't follow because it would cannibalize their existing business?
- **Engineering**: Engineering choices can be Barrier-building tools: high switching costs from deep integration and a sticky data model, or network economies from an open API/ecosystem, are moats engineering can actively construct. When evaluating an architecture, ask: does this design deepen or erode a specific Power?
- **Product**: A high-altitude filter for new features: does this feature raise switching costs (data/workflow lock-in), strengthen network economies (mutual benefit across merchants and buyers), or accumulate a cornered data resource? A feature that is merely "useful but easy to copy" is not a strategic moat.
- **Ops**: Quantify moat strength: measure switching costs via cohort retention and the relationship between integration/data depth and churn; validate network economies via "network size vs per-user value"; validate scale economies via the curve of unit cost falling with scale.
- **Strategy**: The best-fitting moat language for SaaS platforms: a multi-tenant commerce platform can pursue scale economies (shared infrastructure amortizing cost), network economies (merchant × buyer × app ecosystem), and switching costs (data and operational lock-in) at once. Counter-positioning fits especially well against large incumbents — doing the low-price or open model they won't match for fear of self-cannibalization.
- **2026**: In 2025 Helmer appeared on Lenny's Newsletter/Podcast discussing AI's two-sided effect on Power: AI may erode some moats by lowering the cost of imitation, but it also amplifies scale economies, network economies, and data-type cornered resources — so a platform's data and ecosystem advantages can become more valuable, not less.
- Sources: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/business-strategy-with-hamilton-helmer, https://www.sachinrekhi.com/p/7-powers-hamilton-helmer, https://blas.com/7-powers/

### Objectives and Key Results (OKR) · fit 5
*aka / source:* Objectives and Key Results; originated at Intel (Andy Grove), popularized by John Doerr in *Measure What Matters*
- **What it is**: An Objective is a qualitative, inspirational statement of what you want to achieve; Key Results are quantitative, measurable indicators of progress toward it. OKRs earn their value by aligning high-level strategy down to team execution, giving a clear line of sight from a task to organizational strategy. Common principles: 3–5 objectives per level, co-created through iterating between top-down and bottom-up.
- **Use in the process**: Turn strategy (from Rumelt's kernel or Martin's cascade) into executable alignment: company-level Objectives reflect how-to-win, and team-level Key Results are its leading indicators. Use OKRs to arbitrate quarterly priorities — resources go to what moves a Key Result and everything else waits. OKRs are about doing the right things; project management is about doing things right.
- **Questions to ask**: Does this work item genuinely map to a strategic Key Result, or is it just busywork? Are our Key Results leading indicators (they reflect impact early) or lagging ones (they only reflect the past)? Do we have too many OKRs to stay focused (more than 3–5)?
- **Engineering**: Align engineering work to product outcomes rather than output: write Key Results as user-perceivable results — "checkout P99 < X ms," "zero error rate on auto-applying loyalty points" — not "close N tickets." Connect each sprint to a strategic Key Result so engineering doesn't drift away from strategy.
- **Product**: Turn roadmap themes into Objectives and the definition of success into Key Results, then use them as the basis for feature trade-offs and sequencing; cap the number of objectives to force the product team onto the few outcomes that truly matter.
- **Ops**: OKRs directly drive the metrics system: a Key Result must be measurable, so the data/analytics team owns defining the measurement, building tracking dashboards, and using cohort/funnel analysis to validate whether the Key Result is genuinely a leading indicator.
- **Strategy**: OKRs are the antidote to strategy that never lands — translating abstract strategy (vision/where-to-play/how-to-win) layer by layer into trackable, accountable quarterly outcomes that connect company priorities to team execution. But note that OKRs are an alignment-and-execution tool; they cannot replace the upstream strategic choice and diagnosis.
- **2026**: 2025 best practice stresses that OKRs must serve an explicit long-term vision (or they degenerate into a to-do list), be capped at 3–5 to keep focus, and be integrated with project management to form a full line of sight from strategy to task; using OKRs alone with no upstream strategy is a common failure mode.
- Sources: https://quantive.com/resources/articles/okr-guide, https://www.whatmatters.com/okrs-explained/okrs-strategy-and-execution, https://www.celoxis.com/article/okrs-project-management

### Jobs to Be Done (JTBD) · fit 5
*aka / source:* JTBD; Jobs Theory; popularized by Clayton Christensen, with distinct schools from Tony Ulwick (ODI) and Bob Moesta
- **What it is**: JTBD holds that people don't buy products; they "hire" a product to make some kind of progress in a particular circumstance. The focus shifts from "what the product is" to "why the customer uses it." Tony Ulwick originated an early form using Six Sigma thinking around 1990 and introduced it to Christensen; Christensen brought it into his theory of disruptive innovation.
- **Use in the process**: Before product-direction and feature decisions, define the job the customer is really trying to get done and the measures by which they judge success. The decision criterion becomes "does this help the customer get the job done better?" rather than "does a competitor have it?" Also used to redefine a market by job rather than by product category.
- **Questions to ask**: What progress is the customer actually trying to make (not which feature they ask for)? What are they "hiring" today to get this job done — a competitor, a spreadsheet, or a manual workaround? In what circumstance would they "fire" us and hire something else? Are we solving the job, or just stacking features?
- **Engineering**: Helps engineering understand the why behind a requirement, not just the what, leading to technical designs and trade-offs that fit the job; avoids implementing a spec literally while missing the progress the customer is actually after. The job's success measures can be turned into measurable system metrics.
- **Product**: JTBD is at the core of discovery and feature definition: through depth interviews and contextual observation, surface the job a merchant (tenant) is trying to get done (e.g. "open a store that can take payments and ship orders, quickly and painlessly") and design onboarding and features around it rather than accreting isolated features.
- **Ops**: Operationalize the job's "progress" into trackable metrics (e.g. time from signup to first completed sale), use behavioral data to validate whether the product really gets the job done better, and locate the steps where the job stalls.
- **Strategy**: Redefines the competitive arena for a SaaS: viewing the market by "the job merchants need done" rather than "the e-commerce-software category" reveals the true alternatives (self-hosting, manual workarounds, other tools) and blue-ocean opportunities, echoing the value reconstruction of Blue Ocean Strategy.
- **2026**: JTBD has three main schools (Christensen's progress-making theory, Ulwick's Outcome-Driven Innovation/ODI, and Moesta's Switch interview method); as of 2025 it remains a mainstream discovery method for product teams and is increasingly paired with AI-assisted interview analysis to accelerate qualitative insight.
- Sources: https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/, https://strategyn.com/jobs-to-be-done/, https://gopractice.io/product/jobs-to-be-done-the-theory-and-the-frameworks/

### North Star Metric Framework · fit 5
*aka / source:* North Star Metric (NSM); NSM + Input Metrics; popularized by Amplitude (John Cutler) and Reforge
- **What it is**: A framework popularized by Amplitude (John Cutler's *North Star Playbook*) and Reforge: pick a single leading indicator that best captures the value customers get from the product as the North Star Metric, then connect it to a handful of input metrics that the team can directly influence and that together drive the North Star. A good NSM expresses customer value, is a leading indicator (not a lagging one like revenue), and is consistent with strategy.
- **Use in the process**: Use the NSM to align the whole product organization to one definition of value: evaluate any initiative by asking "which input metric does it move, and does that in turn move the North Star?" Input metrics can be assigned to different teams, forming a traceable chain from work to value.
- **Questions to ask**: Does our North Star express customer value, or just our output/revenue? Is it a leading or a lagging indicator? Which few input metrics are levers we can directly move that, summed together, drive the North Star?
- **Engineering**: Helps engineering connect performance/reliability work to value: if the North Star is some form of "successfully completed transaction/experience," then engineering's latency, error rate, and availability are its input metrics, letting infrastructure investment be prioritized in the language of value.
- **Product**: Aligns feature prioritization and experiment design: each experiment points explicitly at an input metric; avoid building "interesting but value-less" features that don't connect to the North Star.
- **Ops**: The NSM framework is fundamentally an analytics framework: the data team owns defining the North Star and input metrics, modeling their causal/correlational links, and using cohorts and A/B tests to validate whether the input metrics genuinely lead the North Star. Most products in fact have acquisition, retention, and monetization dimensions rather than a single North Star.
- **Strategy**: Condenses abstract strategy (how-to-win, the customer value in JTBD) into one company-wide value metric, often paired with OKRs: the North Star defines value, and OKRs define which input to move and how far this quarter.
- **2026**: Reforge cautions "don't let your North Star Metric fool you" — a single metric, if chosen wrong or locally optimized, can mislead the whole company; the 2025 trend is to pair the North Star with a set of guardrail/counter-metrics to avoid chasing one number at the expense of retention or quality.
- Sources: https://amplitude.com/books/north-star/about-north-star-framework, https://www.reforge.com/blog/north-star-metrics, https://www.reforge.com/blog/north-star-metric-growth, https://medium.com/@amplitudeHQ/every-product-needs-a-north-star-8abd3202da6f

### Porter's Five Forces · fit 4
*aka / source:* Five Forces; industry structure analysis; by Michael Porter (1979)
- **What it is**: Michael Porter's tool for analyzing industry attractiveness (profit potential), assessing five forces: threat of new entrants, supplier bargaining power, buyer bargaining power, threat of substitutes, and rivalry among existing competitors. The stronger the forces, the more they compress average industry profitability.
- **Use in the process**: When entering a new market or judging whether a business line is worth investing in, score each of the five forces. If most are high (low entry barriers, easy buyer switching, abundant substitutes), you must either find a structural advantage or stay out. Also used to identify which force you need to build a defense against.
- **Questions to ask**: How easily can customers switch to a competitor (buyer power)? Are low-cost substitutes (e.g. merchants self-hosting open-source commerce) eroding us? How low are the entry barriers for new entrants (headless commerce, AI site-builders)? How dependent are we on key suppliers (cloud, payments, logistics)?
- **Engineering**: Indirect but useful: map "supplier bargaining power" onto critical third-party dependencies (a single payment provider, single cloud, single search vendor), signaling that engineering should build abstraction layers and multi-vendor fault tolerance to reduce lock-in risk.
- **Product**: Evaluate whether to build an integration or open an API: does an open ecosystem (an app-store SDK) lower buyer switching costs, or does it instead invite the threat of substitutes? Helps product weigh the structural consequences of "open vs closed."
- **Ops**: Use churn and switching data to quantify buyer power and substitute threat: rising churn plus more competitor price-comparison searching is a sign the forces are worsening. A rising share of supplier costs reflects increasing supplier bargaining power.
- **Strategy**: The opening move for diagnosing e-commerce-SaaS industry structure: identify whether the whole space is being pushed toward lower profitability by "headless / AI-site-builder / platform-style commerce," then decide whether to go deep in a vertical (focus) or build ecosystem stickiness to counter the forces.
- **2026**: Several 2025 analyses note that Five Forces has limits in the AI/platform-ecosystem era: it underweights strategic alliances, data-driven collaboration, and ecosystems, and the blurring of industry boundaries. In practice it is often complemented by 7 Powers or an ecosystem lens rather than used alone.
- Sources: https://www.mtlc.co/revising-porters-five-forces-analysis-in-the-age-of-ai/, https://www.aijbm.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/L82114118.pdf, https://www.bobstanke.com/blog/porters-five-forces-vs-porters-generic-strategies

### Porter's Generic Strategies · fit 4
*aka / source:* cost leadership / differentiation / focus; Generic Strategies; by Michael Porter (1980)
- **What it is**: Porter argues that a firm has essentially three coherent competitive positions: cost leadership, differentiation, and focus (which subdivides into cost focus and differentiation focus). Porter warns that pursuing two at once leaves you "stuck in the middle" with no advantage.
- **Use in the process**: For major investment, pricing, or feature trade-offs, first confirm which position you've chosen: if you pursue differentiation (best experience, ecosystem), don't self-harm in a price war; if you pursue focus (a vertical commerce niche), don't take every segment. Use it to check whether the firm's behavior stays consistent with its position.
- **Questions to ask**: What exactly do we win on — cheaper, more distinctive, or more focused on a particular segment? Are we "stuck in the middle," neither the cheapest nor distinctive enough? Could a given price cut or feature addition break the consistency of our position?
- **Engineering**: If the firm pursues cost leadership, the engineering goal is lowering per-tenant unit cost (shared infrastructure, multi-tenant resource efficiency, automated operations); if it pursues differentiation, engineering invests in hard-to-copy experience quality and feature depth. The position determines which objective function you optimize.
- **Product**: A positioning check on feature trade-offs: under differentiation, invest in a few deep, distinctive features; under cost/focus, build a just-enough, scalable feature set. Avoid burning at both ends.
- **Ops**: A cost-leadership position must watch unit economics closely (per-tenant COGS, gross margin); a differentiation position watches willingness-to-pay premium, retention, and NPS. The metrics system should differ by the chosen generic strategy.
- **Strategy**: Clarifies a SaaS's overall competitive posture: is it the "most cost-effective full-feature platform," the "premium-brand experience," or the "system that understands a given vertical (beauty / fresh grocery) best"? 2025 research finds differentiation and focus are significantly positively associated with performance, while pure cost leadership in SMB SaaS often leads to unsustainably low margins.
- **2026**: Recent empirical work (2017, 2025) shows differentiation and focus strategies have a significant positive effect on firm performance, while pure cost leadership in many settings fails to improve organizational performance — a warning sign for SaaS that competes on low price alone.
- Sources: https://www.goodfellowpublishers.com/free_files/Chapter%209-c8b5a75f8385aa2809aeb26fcc39d9ba.pdf, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/316219649_Five_Competitive_Forces_Model_and_the_Implementation_of_Porter's_Generic_Strategies_to_Gain_Firm_Performances, https://www.bobstanke.com/blog/porters-five-forces-vs-porters-generic-strategies

### Blue Ocean Strategy and the ERRC Grid · fit 4
*aka / source:* Blue Ocean Strategy; Four Actions Framework; Eliminate-Reduce-Raise-Create Grid; by W. Chan Kim & Renée Mauborgne (2005)
- **What it is**: Kim and Mauborgne argue against competing in bloody red oceans and instead pursuing value innovation — opening up uncontested blue oceans by simultaneously raising buyer value and lowering cost. The ERRC grid achieves this through the Four Actions Framework: Eliminate factors the industry has long competed on but that are actually superfluous; Reduce factors that have been over-served, well below the industry standard; Raise the under-served factors well above the industry standard; and Create factors the industry has never offered. Eliminate and Reduce lower cost; Raise and Create lift buyer value.
- **Use in the process**: When repositioning a product or business model, list the competitive factors "everyone competes on" and run each through ERRC. The key is to move in both directions at once (lower cost and raise value), not just Create/Raise — which would over-engineer and inflate the cost structure.
- **Questions to ask**: Which factors that the industry takes for granted, but customers don't actually care about, can we Eliminate? Which have we over-invested in and can Reduce? Is there a factor the industry has never offered that, if Created, would change the rules of the game? Are we only adding features (only Raise/Create), pushing costs ever higher?
- **Engineering**: ERRC maps directly onto product-architecture simplification: Eliminate legacy features/config options nobody uses that drag down development and operations; Reduce options whose over-configurability creates complexity; Raise/Create engineering investment toward genuinely differentiating experiences, lowering both cognitive and operational cost.
- **Product**: A tool for redesigning a module or pricing plan: apply Eliminate/Reduce to existing feature bloat (echoing the "avoid over-configurability" spirit of the admin-rules reference), and reinvest the saved effort to Create an integration or automation competitors lack — achieving "simpler and more valuable at once."
- **Ops**: Drive ERRC with feature-usage data: low usage plus high maintenance cost → Eliminate candidate; high configuration complexity but rarely adjusted → Reduce candidate. Use customer interviews to surface the unmet need worth Creating.
- **Strategy**: Helps an e-commerce SaaS escape the red ocean of "comparing feature lists with competitors": for instance, recompose the value curve for a particular vertical — eliminate the bloat of large platforms, create workflows unique to that vertical — opening a small but uncontested blue ocean.
- **2026**: In 2024–2025, ERRC is frequently used to reshape product lines with AI: Create entirely new automation value while Eliminating/Reducing labor-intensive or redundant legacy features — which aligns neatly with value innovation's "raise value and lower cost at once."
- Sources: https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/tools/errc-grid/, https://www.blueoceanstrategy.com/blog/errc-grid-template-examples/, https://medium.com/@pranavbhatblog/errc-grid-for-blue-ocean-strategy-explained-with-case-studies-7dabf70b1a44

### Scenario Planning · fit 4
*aka / source:* Scenario Planning; Shell Method; 2x2 Scenario Matrix; originated at Royal Dutch Shell (1970s)
- **What it is**: A method originated at Royal Dutch Shell (developed by Pierre Wack, Arie de Geus, and others in the 1970s): rather than forecasting a single future, explore several plausible futures. A common approach finds the two most important and most uncertain driving forces, builds a 2×2 matrix, writes each quadrant as a named future narrative, and then stress-tests the current strategy in each world.
- **Use in the process**: For high-uncertainty, high-stakes decisions (betting on AI, expanding cross-border, how open to make the platform), don't rely on a single base case. Build 3–4 scenarios, identify the no-regret moves that hold up across all scenarios and do those first, and set early-warning indicators and contingency strategies for each scenario.
- **Questions to ask**: What are the two variables most critical to our future and least within our control? If regulation / AI / inflation moves to an extreme, does our current strategy still hold? Which actions are worth doing in every future (no-regret)? Which are bets that only pay off in a specific future?
- **Engineering**: A stress test for architecture decisions: treat "10× scale growth," "primary cloud raises prices or goes dark," and "AI traffic surges" as scenarios and check whether the current architecture breaks in each. No-regret moves (portability, horizontal capacity scaling) come first; scenario-specific work is kept as triggerable options.
- **Product**: Antifragile the roadmap: devise a product response for scenarios like "an AI assistant becomes the primary shopping entry point" or "a wave of merchants self-build," identify the foundational capabilities worth building across all scenarios and schedule those first, and avoid betting all resources on a single future.
- **Ops**: Turn scenarios into a dashboard of monitorable early-warning indicators: e.g. the trigger signal for one scenario is "AI-referred traffic share > X%," and once the data approaches it, you activate the corresponding strategy.
- **Strategy**: Standard equipment for long-horizon e-commerce-SaaS strategy: build scenarios for uncertainties like AI, cross-border regulation, and platform-ecosystem evolution, shifting the board's discussion from "predicting who is right" to "we survive in any future, and win more in the favorable ones."
- **2026**: In 2025, bodies like the World Economic Forum (WEF) promoted more agile, gamified scenario exercises; amid extreme AI uncertainty, scenario planning and real options are often combined — scenarios bound the possible worlds, and real options decide how to stage the bets.
- Sources: https://www.weforum.org/stories/2025/02/scenario-game-navigate-uncertainty-and-develop-foresight/, https://www.managementcentre.co.uk/blog/strategy-for-an-uncertain-future-scenario-planning/, https://oboe.com/learn/strategic-foresight-methods-rv4ijq/scenario-planning-techniques-2

### Real Options Thinking · fit 4
*aka / source:* Real Options; Real Options Valuation (ROV); staged investment; from Stewart Myers (1977) and Trigeorgis and others
- **What it is**: Applies the financial-option concept to real investments: under uncertainty, managerial flexibility itself has value. Strategic choices can be treated as options — to defer, to stage/compound (each stage is an option on the next), to expand, to contract/abandon, or to switch. Traditional DCF fails to capture this flexibility value.
- **Use in the process**: For large investments, don't ask "all-in now or not at all"; design it as "spend a little to buy an option, then decide whether to exercise once uncertainty resolves." Distinguish explicitly: the value of deferring vs the learning and positioning value of early commitment. Set abandonment conditions so a failing investment can exit cheaply.
- **Questions to ask**: Can we pay a small amount now to buy the right to decide later, rather than fully committing today? Does this project have a built-in option to scale up if it succeeds and stop if it fails? Have we set an abandonment threshold, or will we keep going because of sunk cost?
- **Engineering**: A perfect fit for spikes / PoCs / feature flags / progressive rollout: do a cheap technical validation first (buy the option), invest fully if the data is good (expand), and discard it if not (abandon). Reversible architecture decisions deserve to be made fast; irreversible ones (database selection, an external API contract) are worth paying more to preserve the option to defer or switch.
- **Product**: An MVP and staged release is itself a real option: validate the demand hypothesis with minimal investment and treat subsequent development as an option to scale up or stop. Avoid one-shot bets on a single large feature; structure it as a series of "stage gate" investments.
- **Ops**: For each stage gate, set a measurable exercise/abandon criterion (advance only when retention, conversion, and unit economics clear a bar), quantifying "learning" into the data signal that decides whether to exercise the option.
- **Strategy**: Betting discipline for entering a new market or product line: test the water at low cost first (a regional pilot, a single vertical) and preserve the flexibility to expand on success and contract on failure. Research (including 2025 China-based empirical work) finds firms that adopt real-options-style staged investment have better long-run performance.
- **2026**: Amid the high AI uncertainty of 2025–2026, real-options thinking (test cheaply, preserve flexibility, set abandonment thresholds) is seen as a more robust AI-investment discipline than one-shot big bets; it is often paired with scenario planning — scenarios bound the uncertainty, and options decide the pace of betting.

### Ansoff Matrix · fit 4
*aka / source:* Ansoff Matrix; Product-Market Growth Matrix; by Igor Ansoff (1957)
- **What it is**: Igor Ansoff's (1957) 2×2 growth-direction matrix, splitting "product (existing/new) × market (existing/new)" into four cells: market penetration (existing product, existing market — lowest risk), market development (existing product into a new market), product development (new product into an existing market), and diversification (new product + new market — highest risk).
- **Use in the process**: When planning the next growth move, map candidate initiatives onto the four cells and sequence resources by risk: usually do market penetration first (optimize acquisition/retention), then market or product development, and only touch diversification when the core is stable and you have slack.
- **Questions to ask**: Is this growth opportunity fundamentally "go deeper with the existing segment," "enter a new market," "ship a new product to the existing segment," or "an entirely new arena"? Are we jumping to high-risk diversification before the core is solid? Does the risk match our tolerance?
- **Engineering**: Helps engineering read the growth intent behind a requirement and judge how much can be reused: penetration/product development usually reuse the existing multi-tenant architecture; market development (cross-border, new locales) needs i18n, compliance, and payment/logistics localization investment; diversification may demand an entirely new system.
- **Product**: A positioning and prioritization tool for new features/modules: advanced features for the existing segment (product development) vs reaching a new segment (market development), with investment and risk set accordingly.
- **Ops**: The market-penetration cell maps directly onto your most familiar data work: lowering churn, lifting conversion and ARPU, optimizing onboarding. New markets/products require establishing new baseline success metrics.
- **Strategy**: A clear framework for an e-commerce SaaS's growth path: the typical trajectory is penetration (optimize acquisition and retention of existing merchants) → market development (internationalization, new segments) → product development (new modules like POS, checkout, SDK) → and only diversification once cash flow is stable.
- **2026**: Multiple 2026 guides still treat Ansoff as a practical skeleton for SaaS GTM and growth planning; a common pairing uses Ansoff to set direction and real options / Three Horizons to set the pace of betting and the risk layering.

### Three Horizons of Growth · fit 3
*aka / source:* McKinsey Three Horizons; 3 Horizons; by Baghai, Coley & White, *The Alchemy of Growth* (2000)
- **What it is**: A growth-portfolio model from McKinsey partners Baghai, Coley, and White in their 2000 book *The Alchemy of Growth*, layering by maturity and time-to-payoff: Horizon 1 (extend and defend the core business), Horizon 2 (build emerging growth engines), and Horizon 3 (create viable options for the future). The three horizons need different cultures and KPIs, and should be run in parallel rather than handed off sequentially.
- **Use in the process**: When allocating resources across the portfolio, label which horizon each initiative belongs to and ensure all three are funded rather than everything piled into H1. The key discipline: don't judge H3 exploratory bets by H1's quarterly-profit standards, or you'll kill innovation in the cradle.
- **Questions to ask**: Is all our investment crammed into H1 (just tending the existing business) while H2/H3 starve? What different success metric should each horizon be judged by? Which H3 bets are being killed unfairly because they're judged by H1's ROI standard?
- **Engineering**: Maps onto engineering resource allocation: H1 = maintaining and incrementally optimizing existing systems; H2 = new platform capabilities/refactors; H3 = exploratory technology (new AI capabilities, new-architecture PoCs). Reminds the team to reserve a share for H2/H3 rather than spending 100% on firefighting and small fixes.
- **Product**: Balance the product portfolio: H1 is polishing core commerce features, H2 is new modules (POS, cross-border), H3 is experimental bets (an AI shopping agent). Ensure the roadmap isn't only H1 increments.
- **Ops**: Set different metrics per horizon: H1 on efficiency and profit, H2 on adoption rate and the growth curve, H3 on learning milestones and hypothesis validation rather than revenue. Avoid judging everything off a single revenue dashboard.
- **Strategy**: Gives SaaS leadership a shared language for the growth narrative and resource allocation, balancing "defend vs explore."
- **2026**: A notable 2025 critique (e.g. from Steve Blank): in the digital/AI era the time axis collapses — an H3 disruption (such as generative AI) can become a core H1 threat within months, not decades. So the three horizons should be run in parallel and re-evaluated quickly, not treated as a fixed time sequence.
