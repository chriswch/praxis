### Online Controlled Experiment / A/B Test (OCE) · fit 5
*aka / source:* A/B test, randomized controlled experiment, split test; Kohavi et al. call it the OCE
- **What it is**: Randomly assign users to a Control (status quo) and one or more Treatment variants. Because randomization makes the groups equivalent *in expectation*, the *expected* difference between groups equals the average causal effect; in any single experiment an observed difference *estimates* that effect but must be separated from sampling noise with a significance / confidence-interval test before it is treated as real (randomization removes confounding in expectation, not within one sample). It is the gold-standard tool for establishing causality.
- **Use in the process**: Frame every product/engineering decision as a measurable hypothesis: write down the OEC and guardrails first, run a power analysis to estimate the required sample size and duration, run for at least one to two full business cycles, then ship/no-ship by a pre-registered decision rule rather than by whether things "feel" better.
- **Questions to ask**: What is my counterfactual (what would have happened without the change)? Is the randomization unit a user, a session, or a shop? Is the effect real or an artifact of the assignment itself (check SRM first)? How large a lift is worth shipping and maintaining this code for?
- **Engineering**: Use a feature flag (e.g. LaunchDarkly or a homegrown flag) for consistent assignment: hash the same user_id into a stable bucket so the user sees the same variant across requests and devices. When canarying (a new checkout API, a new search ranker), treat the "5% traffic ramp" as a controlled experiment for performance and correctness too, monitoring error rate and latency as guardrails.
- **Product**: Ship new features (e.g. a redesigned product-listing flow in the merchant admin, a cart recommendation module) behind an A/B by default: prove with the experiment that it actually lifts conversion rather than relying on PM intuition. In a multi-tenant setting you can randomize at the merchant (shop) level or the buyer level depending on the feature's blast radius.
- **Ops**: Make "conversion / average order value / add-on rate for variant A vs B" a standard dashboard, annotated with confidence intervals and significance, so ops never draws conclusions from single-day noise; set up post-launch tracking to check whether the realized effect matches what the experiment predicted.
- **Strategy**: Build an "experimentation culture" as an organizational moat: ship decisions carry an evidence chain, reducing HiPPO (highest-paid person's opinion) dominance. Kohavi notes that most ideas turn out neutral or negative when tested; institutionalizing A/B testing avoids pouring resources into ineffective changes.
- **2026**: By 2025–2026, experimentation platforms (Statsig, Eppo, GrowthBook, Optimizely) ship CUPED, sequential testing, and SRM checks as defaults. For low-traffic merchants, the field has begun using LLM agents to simulate users (e.g. AgentA/B on arXiv) for early-stage screening when real traffic is insufficient — but a real experiment is still required to confirm.
- Sources: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/trustworthy-online-controlled-experiments/metrics-for-experimentation-and-the-overall-evaluation-criterion/4EA73D169EC43B58991D6824717E8FD3, https://experimentguide.com/, https://www.amazon.com/Trustworthy-Online-Controlled-Experiments-Practical/dp/1108724264

### Overall Evaluation Criterion (OEC) · fit 5
*aka / source:* primary metric, success metric, decision metric; often the measurable proxy for the North Star
- **What it is**: The single (or weighted-combination) quantitative criterion that decides whether an experiment succeeded. It must be measurable within the experiment's short horizon and believed to be causally linked to the long-term strategic goal. It solves the problem of "which number do I actually use to conclude an experiment?"
- **Use in the process**: Each experiment has exactly one OEC, fixed up front in the PRD / experiment plan. If the change moves the OEC up significantly without violating any guardrail, ship. The OEC replaces the bad habit of watching ten metrics at once and cherry-picking the one that flatters you.
- **Questions to ask**: Does this short-term metric truly stand for long-term value (e.g. "clicks" vs "orders completed and not returned within 30 days")? Can it be gamed (e.g. making a button annoying to inflate clicks)? Should the OEC be a buyer-behavior metric or merchant retention?
- **Engineering**: Define the OEC as an event that can be computed precisely from instrumentation (e.g. checkout_success rather than a vague "active"), and guarantee at the instrumentation layer that events are neither duplicated nor dropped — otherwise the OEC itself is untrustworthy.
- **Product**: For a checkout redesign, use "share of sessions that complete payment" rather than "number of people who reach the checkout page"; for a search redesign use "post-search add-to-cart rate" to avoid being misled by intermediate vanity metrics.
- **Ops**: Build a layered metric hierarchy for the company/team: a company-level North Star (e.g. net GMV, merchant retention), a team-level OEC (a leading indicator), and an experiment-level OEC — ensuring each layer is a trustworthy leading indicator of the one above it.
- **Strategy**: The OEC is the key that translates strategy into experimentable language. Choosing the wrong OEC systematically optimizes the entire experimentation system in the wrong direction (e.g. chasing GMV at the expense of merchant health) — strategically, it hands the steering wheel to the wrong number.
- **2026**: The industry trend (Eppo, ABsmartly) is to manage the OEC, guardrails, and counter-metrics as explicitly separated tiers, and to use "can this metric be gamed?" as a design check. Weights for a combined OEC are increasingly calibrated against historical experiment data.
- Sources: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/trustworthy-online-controlled-experiments/metrics-for-experimentation-and-the-overall-evaluation-criterion/4EA73D169EC43B58991D6824717E8FD3, https://absmartly.framer.website/blog/what-are-the-overall-evaluation-criteria, https://siftfeed.com/guides/north-star-guardrail-metrics

### Guardrail Metrics · fit 5
*aka / source:* counter metrics, non-inferiority metrics, protection metrics
- **What it is**: Secondary metrics paired with the OEC to ensure a "winning" experiment hasn't caused unacceptable harm elsewhere (e.g. OEC up but page latency worsens, complaints rise, returns increase). In essence, it is a non-inferiority check.
- **Use in the process**: Write the ship rule as "OEC up significantly AND no guardrail degraded significantly beyond its tolerance threshold." If a guardrail is violated, do not ship even if the OEC looks great — roll back or rework.
- **Questions to ask**: To move this metric, what am I most likely sacrificing? Which metrics are "must never get worse" floors (latency, crash rate, refund rate, merchant complaints)? What tolerance interval do I set for each guardrail?
- **Engineering**: Set p95/p99 latency, error rate, crash rate, and API timeout rate as standard guardrails on every experiment, wired into the platform for automatic monitoring; frontend bundle size and time-to-first-render also make good guardrails, preventing new features from slowing the whole site.
- **Product**: In a checkout-optimization experiment, set "payment failure rate," "return rate," and "support ticket volume" as guardrails to prevent introducing deceptive UI just to chase conversion; in a recommendation-module experiment, set "catalog diversity / long-tail exposure" as a guardrail to avoid pushing only best-sellers and harming the ecosystem.
- **Ops**: Establish a shared cross-experiment guardrail suite (organization-level guardrails) so every team's experiment automatically inherits the company's health floors, letting ops see at a glance which experiments crossed a line.
- **Strategy**: Guardrails are the mechanism for internalizing long-term and cross-team externalities into a single experiment's decision, preventing local optimization from harming the whole (e.g. one team chasing GMV while driving up shipping cost and returns). They are the strategic tool for balancing growth against health.
- **2026**: Modern platforms split guardrails into "trust guardrails (e.g. SRM, sample size)" and "business/quality guardrails." Since 2025 it is common to use a non-inferiority statistical test (rather than only checking for significance) to decide whether something "did not get worse."
- Sources: https://www.geteppo.com/blog/counter-metrics, https://siftfeed.com/guides/north-star-guardrail-metrics, https://absmartly.framer.website/blog/what-are-the-overall-evaluation-criteria

### Sample Size, Power & Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE) · fit 5
*aka / source:* power analysis, statistical power, minimum detectable effect, a priori power analysis
- **What it is**: The pre-launch math: from the baseline conversion rate, the MDE (the smallest lift you want to detect), the significance level α (often 0.05), and the desired power (often 0.8), solve for the sample size per arm and the duration. Power = 0.8 means that if a true effect exists, you have an 80% chance of detecting it (a 20% miss rate = Type II error).
- **Use in the process**: Before launching, compute: given current traffic, how many days are needed to detect the "smallest commercially meaningful lift"? If it would take six months to reach power, the change isn't worth validating by A/B (insufficient traffic) — switch to another method or make a bolder change instead.
- **Questions to ask**: How small a lift is commercially meaningful to me (that is the MDE — smaller is not better)? Do I have enough traffic to detect it in a reasonable time? Am I running an underpowered experiment chasing a tiny effect I am doomed not to see?
- **Engineering**: Automate the power calculation into the experiment-creation flow, in the platform or via statsmodels / a homegrown script; for low-traffic merchants, automatically flag "this experiment is underpowered" so engineers don't waste time on tests that can never reach significance.
- **Product**: At feature-kickoff, use power analysis to decide whether the feature can even be validated by A/B: a high-traffic buyer-side feature can; a low-frequency merchant-admin feature (inherently small sample) may instead need CUPED, qualitative methods, or a geo design.
- **Ops**: Maintain a baseline table of conversion rates and traffic per surface so anyone estimating an experiment's duration has reliable inputs; afterward, check whether realized power matched expectations (to catch underpowered runs caused by a mis-estimated baseline).
- **Strategy**: Power planning sets the organization's "experiment throughput": traffic is a scarce resource, and understanding the MDE lets strategy allocate limited traffic to the bets worth validating with effects large enough to see, rather than spreading it across a pile of micro-tweaks.
- **2026**: For a two-proportion test, n ≈ (Z_α/2 + Z_β)² × (p1(1−p1) + p2(1−p2)) / (p1−p2)², where 95% corresponds to 1.96 and 80% power to 0.84. Variance-reduction techniques such as CUPED can materially lower the required n (equivalent to adding traffic) and are now the standard remedy for low traffic.
- Sources: https://cxl.com/blog/statistical-power/, https://www.statsig.com/calculator, https://www.abtasty.com/blog/sample-size-calculation/

### CUPED Variance Reduction · fit 5
*aka / source:* Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data; related: CUPAC, regression adjustment, stratification
- **What it is**: Use pre-experiment data as a covariate to remove the "predictable noise" in a metric that existing behavior can linearly explain, shrinking variance and raising sensitivity. Mathematically it subtracts θ·(X − mean X), where θ = Cov(Y,X)/Var(X) — a *mean-preserving recentering* (not a raw regression residual, which would shift the level) that removes covariate-explained variance while leaving the metric's mean, and thus the treatment-effect estimate, unbiased — provided the covariate is strictly pre-treatment and independent of assignment. Variance shrinks to Var × (1 − ρ²), where ρ is the correlation between metric and covariate.
- **Use in the process**: Apply CUPED to high-variance metrics (revenue, average order value): it is equivalent to adding traffic and shortens the days needed to reach significance. This makes otherwise-underpowered experiments feasible, or lets running ones conclude faster.
- **Questions to ask**: Do I have per-user pre-experiment behavior to use as a covariate? Does that covariate have high explanatory power (correlation) with the outcome? Is the covariate strictly from before the experiment and unaffected by the treatment (otherwise it introduces bias)?
- **Engineering**: Add a step to the analysis pipeline: join each user's pre-period value of the same metric (e.g. the prior 4 weeks of spend) and apply the CUPED adjustment; ensure the covariate is drawn from before the assignment time and is uncontaminated by the treatment, or the conclusion is invalid.
- **Product**: Lets low-traffic merchant-admin features and high-variance revenue experiments conclude in a reasonable time, indirectly raising the product team's experiment throughput and iteration speed.
- **Ops**: Make CUPED the default analysis for revenue / AOV-type experiments, and report the "effective traffic multiplier" to ops so everyone understands how much duration was saved.
- **Strategy**: Variance reduction is a strategic lever that trades "data engineering" for "traffic / time": for a traffic-constrained multi-tenant commerce platform (most merchants have modest traffic), it directly multiplies the number of experiments the whole organization can run.
- **2026**: Introduced by Deng, Xu, Kohavi & Walker at WSDM 2013; the original paper reports roughly 50% variance reduction (the same power with about half the users). A covariate with ρ² ≈ 0.4 yields about a 1.66x effective-traffic multiplier (its effect on statistical *power* depends on the effect size and operating point, so quote the variance / effective-n multiplier as the design-invariant figure rather than a single power-gain number). CUPED has since evolved into CUPAC (using ML-predicted values as the covariate) and combinations with trimmed means for robustness, and is now standard at large tech firms.
- Sources: https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2433396.2433413, https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/group/experimentation-platform-exp/articles/deep-dive-into-variance-reduction/, https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013-02-CUPED-ImprovingSensitivityOfControlledExperiments.pdf

### Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM) · fit 5
*aka / source:* SRM, sample ratio mismatch, randomization check
- **What it is**: A statistically significant deviation between the observed group ratio and the designed ratio (e.g. 50/50). Kohavi likens it to a fever: not the disease itself, but a symptom of any of several data-quality problems (faulty assignment, telemetry loss, bots, asymmetric processing-stage filtering).
- **Use in the process**: Before looking at any OEC or effect, run an SRM test (typically chi-squared). If the p-value is extremely small (e.g. < 0.001), the assignment is contaminated, every effect number is untrustworthy, and you must debug rather than conclude.
- **Questions to ask**: Does my assignment ratio match expectation? Are bots, crawlers, or a variant's dropped instrumentation "eating" users from one group? Is the filtering / de-duplication logic asymmetric across the two groups?
- **Engineering**: Make the SRM check an automatic gate in the platform (run daily): compare exposure-event counts against the allocation ratio. Common root causes are a redirect that loses users on the treatment side, lossy instrumentation, or caching that leaves some users unmarked.
- **Product**: When a new feature shows "abnormally high treatment conversion," suspect SRM before celebrating — likely some class of low-engagement users went unlogged in treatment, manufacturing a fake lift.
- **Ops**: Make SRM the first red/green light on the experiment report; when ops sees an SRM red light, it knows the data can't drive a decision, avoiding action on dirty data.
- **Strategy**: SRM is the gatekeeper of the whole experimentation system's trustworthiness; an organization that doesn't check SRM has every experiment conclusion discounted. Institutionalizing SRM is a prerequisite for a trustworthy experimentation culture.
- **2026**: Automatic SRM detection is now standardized (Microsoft's KDD 2019 paper "Diagnosing Sample Ratio Mismatch"); by 2025 most platforms ship SRM checks with root-cause classifiers, extended to detecting pre-experiment bias and assignment-system faults.
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_ratio_mismatch, https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3292500.3330722, https://medium.com/@deepti.agl16/part-i-trustworthy-online-controlled-experiments-a-b-testing-twymans-law-7dc5032073c7

### Peeking, P-hacking & Multiple Comparisons · fit 5
*aka / source:* peeking problem, optional stopping, p-hacking, multiple comparisons problem, family-wise error; corrections: Bonferroni, Benjamini–Hochberg (FDR)
- **What it is**: A cluster of anti-patterns that systematically manufacture false positives. Peeking / optional stopping: repeatedly checking a fixed-sample experiment and stopping the moment it's significant. P-hacking: tweaking parameters / windows until p < 0.05. Multiple comparisons: testing many metrics / segments at once, inflating the chance that at least one is significant by luck.
- **Use in the process**: For a fixed-sample experiment, "run it to completion before looking"; if you must look mid-flight, switch to a sequential test. When testing many metrics / segments, apply a Bonferroni or FDR correction. Pre-register the hypothesis and OEC, and don't cherry-pick the slice that favors you afterward.
- **Questions to ask**: Am I stopping because "I saw significance and wanted to"? How many metrics / segments did I test at once (corrected or not)? Was this significant result the pre-registered hypothesis, or something I dredged up after the fact?
- **Engineering**: Have the platform default to "don't show a significance verdict before the target sample is reached," or switch to always-valid inference, blocking naive peeking at the tool layer; auto-apply FDR correction on multi-metric dashboards so engineers don't change code on a chance significance.
- **Product**: Forbid the "it looked like a winner on day three so we shipped" workflow; when a PM wants to inspect N segment results, require declaring the primary segment up front and marking the rest exploratory (exploratory findings need a fresh confirmatory experiment).
- **Ops**: In self-serve analytics, surface a reminder: testing 20 metrics simultaneously, each at α = 0.05, yields roughly a 64% chance of at least one false positive; have reports flag which conclusions had a multiple-comparisons correction applied.
- **Strategy**: These traps are the main reason experimentation can "look useful while actually producing noise"; ungoverned, an organization accumulates a pile of "success stories" built on false positives, eroding trust in data and the correctness of resource allocation over time.
- **2026**: The mainstream 2025 remedy is tool-layer enforcement: platforms default to sequential testing / Bayesian shrinkage to neutralize peeking and auto-apply FDR across many metrics. Kohavi and others keep stressing that pre-registration and "one OEC" are the most effective institutional safeguards.
- Sources: https://docs.growthbook.io/using/experimentation-problems, https://www.mida.so/ab-testing-terms/simpsons-paradox, https://nebulab.com/blog/ecommerce-ab-testing-mistakes

### Simpson's Paradox, Twyman's Law, Novelty/Primacy Effects · fit 5
*aka / source:* Simpson's paradox (aggregation reversal), Twyman's law (too good to be true), novelty effect, primacy effect, winner's curse
- **What it is**: Three interpretation-layer traps. Simpson's paradox: an aggregate trend reverses after segmenting (a confounder is unevenly distributed across groups). Twyman's law: "the more interesting or surprising the data, the more likely it is wrong." Novelty effect: a new feature is inflated early by curious early adopters, then decays. Primacy effect: existing users resist the new version and are penalized at first.
- **Use in the process**: On a striking result, first assume "it's a bug, not a breakthrough" (Twyman) and recheck instrumentation / SRM; when aggregate and per-segment conclusions disagree, hunt the confounder; when novelty/primacy is plausible, extend the experiment or use a long-term holdout to see whether the effect persists.
- **Questions to ask**: This "+40% lift" is too good — is instrumentation double-counting? The overall winner loses in every segment (or vice versa); what confounder am I missing? Is the lift still there a month later, or just curiosity?
- **Engineering**: When Twyman triggers, check the data pipeline first (duplicate events, drops, time zones, asymmetric de-duplication); Simpson's paradox is the reminder to stratify on key dimensions (device, new vs returning, merchant) at assignment time or in post-hoc analysis.
- **Product**: For redesigns that change established habits (nav bar, checkout steps), expect a primacy effect — early data skewing negative is normal — so run long enough; for eye-catching new features (animations, badges), beware novelty and don't call it on week-one data.
- **Ops**: Have reports default to segment drill-down to expose Simpson's paradox; auto-trigger a data-quality recheck on experiments claiming a large lift; for important changes, monitor an "effect-decay-over-time curve" to catch novelty/primacy.
- **Strategy**: These traps make it easy for an organization to mistake short-term or spurious effects for strategic wins; Twyman's discipline (be more skeptical of good news) and a long-term-holdout culture keep strategy from being built on numbers that will evaporate.
- **2026**: Kohavi and others list Twyman's law as a core mindset for experiment trustworthiness; since 2021 there are dedicated long-term-effect estimation methods (e.g. arXiv 2102.12893 "Novelty and Primacy") and surrogate / long-term-holdout frameworks that infer long-run effects from short-run data.
- Sources: https://www.getdalton.com/blogs/simpsons-paradox-ab-testing, https://atticusli.com/replication-crisis/ab-testing-twymans-law/, https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.12893

### Frequentist vs Bayesian A/B Testing · fit 4
*aka / source:* p-value vs probability-to-be-best; NHST vs Bayesian decision
- **What it is**: Two interpretation-and-decision frameworks. The split is about the *definition of probability* (long-run frequency vs degree of belief), **not** fixed vs sequential sampling — sequential / SPRT / always-valid designs are themselves frequentist. The frequentist view outputs p-values / confidence intervals; in its standard *fixed-horizon* form it requires a pre-set sample size (frequentist sequential methods relax this — see Sequential Testing below). The Bayesian view combines a prior with the data into a posterior, and outputs "the probability B beats A" and "expected loss" — more intuitive for non-statisticians.
- **Use in the process**: Frequentist (fixed-horizon): run to the pre-set sample size, then conclude only if p < 0.05 — or switch to a frequentist sequential design if you need to peek. Bayesian: set a decision threshold (e.g. "probability B beats A ≥ 95%" or "expected loss < tolerance") and ship, with the bonus of directly answering "how much do I expect to gain."
- **Questions to ask**: Am I answering "is there a difference" (frequentist) or "how likely, and by how much, is B better than A" (Bayesian)? Do I have a reliable historical prior to use? Does the team understand p-values, or is "win probability" easier to communicate?
- **Engineering**: When choosing a platform or building an engine, this is an architecture decision: a Bayesian engine more easily exposes a "win probability" API for self-serve PM dashboards; the frequentist approach integrates more cleanly with a fixed-sample + power workflow. Note that Bayesian inference is not "peek infinitely for free" — a stopping rule still matters.
- **Product**: For results shown to PMs / merchants, Bayesian "B has a 92% probability of being better, expected AOV +3%" communicates the decision better than "p = 0.03"; for high-risk changes (payments) still prefer rigorous frequentist + fixed sample to avoid over-optimism.
- **Ops**: The Bayesian posterior directly hands ops an "effect interval + probability," ideal for ROI estimation; but caution: Bayesian results are sensitive to the prior — too strong a prior shrinks a real effect away (some articles warn Bayesian A/B can fall short in certain settings).
- **Strategy**: The framework choice shapes the whole organization's decision language and risk appetite: Bayesian encourages faster, expected-value-based decisions; frequentist emphasizes controlling false positives. Most mature platforms support both, and the choice should follow the risk of the change.
- **2026**: In 2025–2026 most commercial platforms (Eppo, Statsig, ABsmartly) default to a Bayesian or sequential mode so non-experts can "safely check results anytime"; Bayesian methods are seen as easing peeking, the winner's curse, and multiple testing, though some reflective articles note their limits under small samples or a wrong prior.
- Sources: https://www.geteppo.com/blog/comparing-frequentist-vs-bayesian-approaches, https://www.convert.com/blog/a-b-testing/frequentist-vs-bayesian-ab-testing/, https://medium.com/data-science-collective/tldr-bayesian-a-b-testing-falls-short-f8646529a47a

### Sequential Testing & Always-Valid p-values · fit 4
*aka / source:* confidence sequences, group sequential testing (GST), SPRT, mSPRT, always-valid inference
- **What it is**: Methods that allow "look as you go, stop anytime" without inflating false positives. Confidence sequences / efficacy and futility boundaries adapt dynamically to the data volume, making every peek legitimate. The family includes group sequential designs (interim analyses scheduled in advance) and fully sequential designs (unlimited looks).
- **Use in the process**: Use sequential testing for high-risk changes or when you want to cut losses fast: ship early when the effect is large enough, or kill a bad variant early. The cost is a max-vs-expected trade-off: the *maximum* sample can exceed a fixed design and the alpha-spending penalty bites hardest when the true effect sits near the MDE; the gain is a smaller *expected* sample when the effect is large or null. The confidence interval at an early stop is also wider.
- **Questions to ask**: Do I need the flexibility to "watch anytime and stop on a big effect," or am I chasing a small effect (where fixed-sample is cheaper)? Will the team wrongly assume sequential testing makes daily peeking cost-free?
- **Engineering**: If you build your own system, sequential testing lets a live dashboard be "safely watched anytime," preventing engineers/PMs from manufacturing false positives via naive peeking; it also suits automated "canary early-stop" (auto-kill the treatment when latency/error spikes).
- **Product**: For high-risk, fast-iteration features (a new checkout, a new payment gateway), sequential testing pulls the plug early when an obvious negative appears, shortening the exposure window of a bad change; for minor UI tweaks it doesn't pay off.
- **Ops**: Draw the dynamic sequential boundaries into the ops dashboard so ops can make real-time decisions on a legitimate footing; educate the team that "the effect estimate at an early stop is biased upward (winner's curse)" and not to treat the early-stop number as the final effect.
- **Strategy**: Sequential testing raises the organization's experiment speed and loss-cutting ability, a culture accelerator for fast-iterating growth SaaS; but recognize strategically that it doesn't save on small effects and emboldens statistically untrained teams to look anytime, so it needs accompanying norms.
- **2026**: By 2025–2026 it is a default or optional mode in most commercial platforms (Statsig, Eppo, Optimizely, ABsmartly), chiefly because it lets non-statistical teams "peek safely"; Netflix and Optimizely have promoted always-valid inference. Caveat: it pays a max-sample / alpha-spending penalty that bites hardest for borderline (near-MDE) effects — not a blanket "slower for small effects."
- Sources: https://www.geteppo.com/blog/sequential-testing, https://www.geteppo.com/blog/comparing-frequentist-vs-bayesian-approaches, https://www.abtasty.com/blog/best-statistical-model-for-ab-testing/

### Multi-Armed Bandits (MAB) · fit 4
*aka / source:* MAB, bandit testing; includes ε-greedy, Thompson sampling, UCB-1
- **What it is**: Algorithms that dynamically trade off exploration and exploitation: learn as you go and route more traffic to the currently better-performing variant, minimizing the regret accumulated during the experiment. ε-greedy explores randomly at a fixed rate; Thompson sampling samples from a Bayesian posterior; UCB-1 uses an upper confidence bound. Because allocation adapts to accumulating outcomes, bandit data are **not i.i.d.** across arms — naive per-arm means are biased and their confidence intervals invalid — so recovering a trustworthy effect size needs inverse-propensity-weighted or adaptively-debiased estimators. That bias (not just unequal traffic) is the precise reason a fixed A/B is preferred when you need a clean causal effect size.
- **Use in the process**: When the "cost during the experiment" is expensive or the opportunity window is short (a promo period, a homepage banner) and you only want the best option rather than a precise effect size, use a bandit to automatically maximize reward; when you need a clean causal effect size and significance, still use a fixed A/B.
- **Questions to ask**: Do I care more about "losing less during the experiment" or "getting a precise, trustworthy effect size"? Will the options drift over time (do I need continued exploration)? Are my traffic and conversion rate enough for the bandit to converge?
- **Engineering**: In a recommendation / ranking / banner service, implement Thompson sampling with online posterior updates; handle delayed feedback (an order may not occur until the next day), batch updates, and cold start. Thompson sampling is noisier; UCB-1 is steadier and more robust to noise.
- **Product**: Use a bandit for "many candidates, route traffic to the best in real time" scenarios — homepage slots, campaign copy, recommendation strategies; still use A/B for causal validation of new features. ε-greedy, with its fixed exploration, has a ceiling on optimization and suits low-budget, stable-conversion, simple situations.
- **Ops**: In a promo period, use a contextual bandit to allocate the best offer to different customer segments in real time; ops must monitor regret and per-arm allocation to keep the algorithm from locking onto a false best too early (ε-greedy in particular is reported to be unstable).
- **Strategy**: A bandit turns "experimentation" into a "continuous optimization engine," suited to high-frequency, short-lived decisions (content / slots / offers); but it sacrifices clean causal measurement, so strategically it should divide labor with A/B: A/B to learn knowledge, bandits to earn revenue.
- **2026**: The 2025 mainstream is Thompson sampling and contextual / personalized bandits, increasingly combined with LLM-driven recommendation. Research indicates ε-greedy is unstable, Thompson sampling is better long-run but noisy, and UCB-1 has better precision and noise robustness; the choice depends on budget and the target distribution.
- Sources: https://cxl.com/blog/bandit-tests/, https://www.inwt-statistics.com/read-blog/multi-armed-bandits-as-an-a-b-testing-solution.html, https://www.researchgate.net/publication/350357541_Comparison_of_Various_Multi-Armed_Bandit_Algorithms_E_-greedy_Thompson_sampling_and_UCB-1_to_Standard_AB_Testing

### Difference-in-Differences (DiD) · fit 4
*aka / source:* DiD, diff-in-diff; extensions: event study, staggered DiD, Callaway–Sant'Anna
- **What it is**: When you can't randomize but have panel data on a treatment group and a control group before and after the treatment, estimate the causal effect as "(treated before-after difference) minus (control before-after difference)," using the control's difference to net out the common time trend. The core premise is parallel trends: absent treatment, the two groups would have moved in parallel.
- **Use in the process**: Use it when a change can only roll out to some merchants / regions / time windows: compare the before-after change of the affected vs unaffected to get a more credible causal estimate than a "naive before-after comparison," supporting whether to roll out fully.
- **Questions to ask**: Were the two groups' trends truly parallel before treatment (plot the pre-trend to check)? Did any other event coincide with the treatment and contaminate the control? Which group is a legitimate control?
- **Engineering**: When a feature can only roll out in batches (by region / shard / version) due to technical constraints, DiD estimates the effect after the fact; you need a well-built event table of "treatment timestamp" and group assignment, and note that a staggered rollout biases classic DiD (use the newer estimators).
- **Product**: When a feature ships first to a specific plan or a specific country's merchants and buyer-level randomization isn't possible, use DiD to estimate this cohort's GMV / retention lift relative to merchants not yet enabled, deciding whether to roll out to all plans.
- **Ops**: Evaluate "non-experimental launches" (policy changes, fee changes, a full UI redesign rolled out by region); always plot the parallel-trends figure over the pre-period, or the conclusion is untrustworthy.
- **Strategy**: DiD lets strategic, un-A/B-testable decisions (pricing policy, market entry, fee changes) carry quasi-causal evidence; it is the key tool for upgrading "retrospective observation" into a "defensible causal claim."
- **2026**: From 2020–2025, econometrics produced a large body of corrections for staggered (differently-timed) DiD (Callaway & Sant'Anna, Sun & Abraham, de Chaisemartin); classic two-way fixed effects is biased under heterogeneous effects. As of 2025 there is still active work on "beyond parallel trends" identification strategies — in practice, use the newer estimators.

### Switchback & Geo/Holdout Experiments · fit 4
*aka / source:* switchback test, time-split test, geo experiment, geo holdout, cluster-randomized; designs for handling network effects / interference
- **What it is**: When the treatment spills over and contaminates the control (network effects / two-sided-market interference, as in matching, pricing, inventory), individual-level A/B is biased. A switchback instead toggles A/B for the whole market by time interval (one version everywhere in a given window); geo / cluster experiments randomize by region / cluster; a long-term holdout permanently withholds the change from a small group to measure the long-run cumulative effect.
- **Use in the process**: When a change affects shared resources or units that interact (matching algorithms, dynamic pricing, inventory, logistics dispatch), individual-level A/B violates the *no-interference* part of SUTVA; switch to a switchback (time toggle) or geo (region toggle) so interference is contained within units and between-unit no-interference becomes plausible — at the cost of estimating a *cluster/market-level* effect (not the individual effect), with weaker power. A long-term holdout verifies whether the effect persists.
- **Questions to ask**: Will the treatment reach the control group through a shared resource / network (interference / spillover)? Should the randomization unit be a user, a time interval, or a region? How long does carryover (the prior interval's effect persisting) last? How long must the holdout run to see the long-run effect?
- **Engineering**: Systems with global interaction — matching / pricing / logistics / search ranking — need an experiment framework that supports time-interval randomization (switchback) and region-cluster randomization (geo), not just user-bucketing; you must handle carryover, variance estimation under autocorrelation, and weaker statistical power.
- **Product**: Use a switchback for two-sided-market features (matching buyers and sellers, shared inventory, real-time delivery); use a geo holdout for regional features (local shipping options, regional promotions); after a major launch, keep a 1–5% long-term holdout to monitor effect decay.
- **Ops**: A geo holdout is the standard tool for measuring the incrementality of marketing / logistics / pricing strategies; ops must understand that a switchback inherently has small sample and weak power, needs a longer run, and must analyze with interference-robust variance estimation.
- **Strategy**: For any platform with network effects (a commerce marketplace, delivery, matching), these designs are the prerequisite for "being able to draw a credible causal conclusion at all"; a long-term holdout is the institutional insurance against novelty effects, ensuring strategy rests on durable value.
- **2026**: Bojinov & Simchi-Levi (HBS) systematized the design and analysis of switchbacks; DoorDash has publicly used roughly 30-minute switch windows, and Lyft/Uber widely use time-split designs. From 2024–2026 there is substantial work on optimal switchback window length, power ("Powerful Switchback Experiments – Or Not?"), and empirical-Bayes designs.
- Sources: https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/WP21-034_20160b13-a86c-4a0d-b6e9-bbae288486c5_c93009c0-8003-43fd-bb1a-012c02d33b98.pdf, https://www.statsig.com/blog/switchback-experiments, https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/experimentation-when-you-cant-ab-test-beyond-testing-shaurya-uppal

### Synthetic Control Method (SCM) · fit 3
*aka / source:* SCM, synthetic control, Abadie's synthetic control; extensions: synthetic DiD, augmented SCM
- **What it is**: When there are only a few (even just one) treated units (e.g. one region, one large merchant, one market), build a "synthetic control" as a weighted combination of untreated units that closely matches the treated unit's pre-treatment trajectory; the post-treatment gap between them is the causal effect.
- **Use in the process**: Use it to evaluate a major change for a single or very few units: e.g. launching a new shipping option in only one country, synthesizing "what would have happened without the launch" from other countries to estimate the lift and decide whether to replicate it in other markets.
- **Questions to ask**: Do I have enough sufficiently-similar untreated units to synthesize a control? Does the synthetic control fit well in the pre-treatment period (pre-fit)? Are there spillovers that affect the donor units too (contaminating the synthetic control)?
- **Engineering**: Suited to evaluating infrastructure or policy changes rolled out by region / data center / a single large tenant, when treated units are too few for DiD or randomization; it requires clean long-run panel metrics.
- **Product**: For a new feature piloted at a single flagship merchant or a single market, use synthetic control to estimate its true incremental effect, avoiding being misled by that market's own seasonality / trend.
- **Ops**: The standard analysis tool for a geo pilot (launching a new feature or adjusting fees in one city / region): synthesize a counterfactual baseline and quantify the pilot's net effect for a go/no-go.
- **Strategy**: SCM lets "large but rare" strategic bets (entering a new market, a single-large-customer policy, regional pricing) carry a quantified counterfactual; it is a source of causal evidence for strategic decisions, especially where an RCT is infeasible.
- **2026**: Post-2021, synthetic DiD (Arkhangelsky et al.) and augmented synthetic control improved poor pre-fit and bias; the method is widely adopted in industry (including ad incrementality measurement and geo experiments), often paired with a geo holdout.
- Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6204967/, https://academic.oup.com/aje/article/193/7/1050/7624199, https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/experimentation-when-you-cant-ab-test-beyond-testing-shaurya-uppal

### Regression Discontinuity Design (RDD) · fit 3
*aka / source:* RDD, regression discontinuity, sharp/fuzzy RDD, threshold design
- **What it is**: When treatment is assigned by whether a continuous running variable crosses a threshold, compare units just on either side of the threshold (nearly equivalent) to estimate the causal effect. The logic: units near the threshold differ in almost nothing except whether they received treatment, so the difference can be attributed to the treatment.
- **Use in the process**: Any rule that "uses a threshold to decide who gets some treatment" can be evaluated with RDD: compare those just above with those just below the threshold to see whether the treatment really causes a behavioral difference, supporting whether to adjust the threshold.
- **Questions to ask**: Is treatment strictly determined by a threshold? Can a unit precisely manipulate which side of the threshold it lands on (if so, RDD breaks)? Is the sample near the threshold large enough to estimate?
- **Engineering**: Use it to evaluate "rule-based thresholds": e.g. "free shipping over NT$1000," "membership-points threshold X for an upgrade," "block when the risk score exceeds a threshold" — compare downstream conversion / retention / fraud rate on either side of the threshold.
- **Product**: For threshold rules like free-shipping minimums, VIP tiers, and discount eligibility, use RDD to estimate the true causal effect of "crossing the threshold" on AOV / repurchase, deciding whether to raise or lower the threshold.
- **Ops**: Analyze the marginal effect of existing threshold policies (free shipping, installment eligibility, credit limits), quantifying the likely impact of "nudging the threshold by one notch" without running a separate experiment.
- **Strategy**: RDD turns the company's existing "threshold rules" into a natural experiment, giving pricing / threshold / eligibility decisions causal evidence — and it can often be done retrospectively on historical data, at low cost.
- **2026**: Cattaneo et al. (2023 Handbook chapter) systematized covariate adjustment, bandwidth selection, and robust inference for RDD; in practice you must run a manipulation test (McCrary) to check for human manipulation near the threshold, and by 2025 mature packages exist (rdrobust).
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regression_discontinuity_design, https://mdcattaneo.github.io/papers/Cattaneo-Keele-Titiunik_2023_HandbookCh.pdf, https://bookdown.org/mike/data_analysis/sec-regression-discontinuity.html

### Instrumental Variables (IV) · fit 3
*aka / source:* IV, instrumental variable, 2SLS (two-stage least squares), LATE, encouragement design
- **What it is**: When there is endogeneity / confounding between treatment and outcome and a direct regression is biased, find an "instrument": it affects the treatment (relevance) but influences the outcome only through the treatment (the exclusion restriction), isolating the treatment's causal effect. It is typically estimated by 2SLS and, assuming no one does the opposite of their encouragement (monotonicity / no defiers), recovers the compliers' local effect (the LATE); without monotonicity the estimate is a sign-ambiguous weighted average over compliers and defiers, not a clean LATE.
- **Use in the process**: When you can only "encourage" rather than "force" users to adopt a feature (intent-to-treat ≠ actual use), use "whether randomly encouraged" as the instrument to estimate the causal effect of "actually using the feature" on the outcome.
- **Questions to ask**: Does my instrument truly affect the outcome only through the treatment (does the exclusion restriction hold — often untestable, only arguable)? Is the instrument-treatment relevance strong enough (a weak instrument biases severely)? Am I estimating an effect for everyone or the compliers' LATE?
- **Engineering**: Encouragement design: randomly push a "try the new feature" prompt to some users (the instrument), and use IV to estimate the causal effect of "actually adopting the feature" from "being prompted," resolving the self-selection bias that "people who adopt on their own were already different."
- **Product**: When a new feature can't be forced on and can only be nudged into adoption (e.g. a new app version, an optional AI recommendation), use IV to estimate the effect on true adopters, avoiding over-estimation (adopters were already high-value users).
- **Ops**: When analyzing "push / email reach → behavior change," use random reach as the instrument to isolate the causal effect of the content itself, rather than being contaminated by "people who open mail were already active."
- **Strategy**: IV lets strategies that "can't be forced, only encouraged" (adoption nudges, behavior-change initiatives) still estimate a causal effect; but because it relies on a hard-to-verify exclusion restriction and risks weak instruments, the conclusion's credibility needs careful argument — treat it strategically as supporting evidence.
- **2026**: Weak instruments and the untestable exclusion restriction remain IV's main criticisms; the 2020s saw ML-IV combinations (DeepIV, double/debiased machine learning, DML), but industry A/B settings more often use its special case "encouragement design" than general IV.
- Sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1410.0163, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6204967/, https://mixtape.scunning.com/09-difference_in_differences

### Surrogate Index / Long-Term Effect Prediction · fit 4
*aka / source:* surrogate metric, surrogate endpoint, statistical surrogacy; surrogacy / Prentice criteria (Prentice, *Statistics in Medicine*, 1989); the **surrogate index** (Athey, Chetty, Imbens & Kang, NBER w26463, 2019; published *Review of Economic Studies*, 2025)
- **What it is**: A short experiment can directly measure only short-horizon outcomes, but the decision often turns on a long-run goal (12-month retention, LTV, lifetime earnings) that won't be observed for months. A surrogate index is a **combination** of short-term outcomes, fit on historical data, whose *predicted* value of the long-term outcome stands in for the unobserved truth — so the average treatment effect on the index estimates the treatment effect on the long-run metric. Validity rests on the **surrogacy / no-direct-effect assumption** (Prentice): the surrogate must *fully mediate* the treatment's effect on the long-term outcome — the long-term outcome must be independent of treatment given the surrogates. The failure mode is a treatment that moves the surrogate but **not** the true goal (a leak around the mediator), which the short experiment cannot detect.
- **Use in the process**: This is an *evidence-gathering* tool for when a long-term holdout (see Switchback & Geo/Holdout, below) would be too slow to inform the decision. Pre-register the long-term OEC, then fit the surrogate index on past experiments/observational panels where *both* short- and long-run outcomes are observed, validate it out-of-sample, and only then read live experiments through it. Treat the index as the *measurement instrument*, not a new optimization target — and keep a small long-term holdout running to back-test it (the holdout validates the surrogate; the surrogate buys speed).
- **Questions to ask**: Does the surrogate plausibly capture *all* of the treatment's pathways to the long-term goal, or could this change move it through a path the index doesn't see? Was the index validated on past treatments *similar to* the one I'm now judging (surrogates don't transport across dissimilar interventions)? Am I measuring with the index, or optimizing toward it — and would teams start gaming it (Goodhart — `references/i-ops-analytics-and-metrics.md`)? Is the speed-up worth the risk versus just waiting?
- **Engineering**: Build the surrogate index as a model trained on the warehouse: predict the 90-day retention/LTV label from the first 7–14 days of behavior (sessions, depth, feature adoption), validate it on held-out past experiments, and expose its predicted treatment effect on the experiment dashboard — flagging it as a *predicted* long-run effect, not a measured one, so engineers don't ship on a surrogate the model was never trained to trust for this kind of change.
- **Product**: A growth team can read a 14-day onboarding experiment through a surrogate index instead of waiting a full retention cycle — but must wire the long-term goal as the OEC (`references/h-experimentation-and-causal.md`) and keep retention/refund-style guardrails (`references/h-experimentation-and-causal.md`) so a change that pumps the surrogate (e.g. an engagement nudge) while quietly hurting churn gets caught before rollout.
- **Ops**: Netflix back-tested a surrogate index built on ~14 days against ~63 days of direct measurement across roughly 200 A/B tests and reported ~95% decision-consistency; Wayfair (Demeter) and Instacart use surrogate indices to read 30–60-day metrics early. Ops should institutionalize the back-test: every quarter, refit and re-validate the index against newly-matured long-term data, and retire it for intervention classes where it stops predicting.
- **Strategy** (clinical — also the cautionary tale): Surrogate endpoints are how medicine decides without waiting decades — but the **CAST trial** (1986–1989) is the canonical failure: antiarrhythmic drugs *suppressed the surrogate* (premature ventricular contractions after a heart attack) yet roughly *doubled* mortality, the true endpoint, and the trial was halted early. The lesson for any strategy resting on a leading indicator: a treatment can move the proxy in the right direction while moving the real goal the wrong way, so a surrogate validated on *one* class of interventions can be lethally wrong for a *new* one.
- **2026**: Surrogate-index methodology moved from econometrics into industry experimentation platforms over 2023–2026 (Netflix, Wayfair, Instacart; the Athey–Chetty–Imbens–Kang paper reached *Review of Economic Studies* in 2025). The AI angle cuts both ways: ML-predicted surrogates (richer behavioral embeddings) tighten the index, but a learned surrogate optimized as a *target* is a textbook Goodhart trap (`references/i-ops-analytics-and-metrics.md`) — current practice keeps the long-run metric as the registered OEC, uses the surrogate only to read effects faster, and validates it against a standing long-term holdout.
- Sources: https://www.nber.org/papers/w26463, https://academic.oup.com/restud/advance-article/doi/10.1093/restud/rdaf087/8268796, https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/sim.4780080407, https://arxiv.org/html/2311.11922v2, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardiac_Arrhythmia_Suppression_Trial

### Target Trial Emulation (TTE) · fit 3
*aka / source:* target trial framework, emulated trial; Hernán & Robins, "Using Big Data to Emulate a Target Trial When a Randomized Trial Is Not Available" (Am J Epidemiol, 2016), and Hernán et al., "Specifying a target trial prevents immortal time bias and other self-inflicted injuries in observational analyses" (J Clin Epidemiol, 2016)
- **What it is**: A protocol-design discipline that *precedes* the observational estimators above (DiD/SCM/RDD/IV, `references/h-experimentation-and-causal.md`). Before touching the data, write down the hypothetical randomized trial you *wish* you could run — its eligibility, treatment strategies, treatment assignment, **time-zero** (when eligibility, assignment, and follow-up all start together), outcome, causal contrast (the estimand), and analysis plan — then emulate each component with observational data. Its central payoff is catching the self-inflicted biases naive observational analyses commit, above all **immortal-time bias** (counting survival before treatment could have started as if it were treated time) and a misaligned time-zero.
- **Use in the process**: It sits in *frame* and *evidence*: the moment someone proposes "let's just pull the logs / records and compare adopters vs non-adopters," force the target-trial protocol first, then pick the estimator (the IV/DiD/SCM/RDD choice becomes the emulation of the *assignment* component). It converts a vague "does X cause Y?" into an explicit estimand with a defensible counterfactual, and surfaces whether you even *can* answer it.
- **Questions to ask**: What is the exact randomized trial we wish we'd run (write its protocol)? When is time-zero, and do eligibility, treatment assignment, and start-of-follow-up coincide there — or are we crediting "immortal" pre-treatment time to the treated? Is treatment defined at baseline, or are we (illegitimately) using post-baseline information to classify the groups? What is the estimand — intention-to-treat or per-protocol — and which confounders must be measured *at* time-zero?
- **Engineering**: Estimating whether enabling a new caching/runtime tier "caused" lower latency from telemetry alone: define time-zero as the deploy event, require services to be eligible (comparable load) at that instant, and classify by the strategy assigned then — not by "services that *ended up* on the tier," which immortal-time-biases the survivors. Use a sequential or clone-censor-weight emulation when adoption trickles in over time.
- **Product**: Estimating a feature's effect from logs without an A/B test: rather than "users who used Feature F retained better" (adopters were already engaged, and pre-adoption active days get wrongly credited), specify the target trial — eligible at first exposure, assigned to "offered F" at time-zero, ITT contrast — then emulate it; this typically collapses an inflated retention "lift" toward the real effect, and tells you when only an actual experiment will do.
- **Ops** (clinical / comparative-effectiveness, the method's origin): Estimating whether a drug started at hospital admission lowers 90-day mortality from EHR data — the classic immortal-time trap is that patients must *survive long enough to receive* the drug, so naive analyses fabricate a survival benefit. The target trial fixes eligibility and time-zero at admission and assigns strategy there, so the spurious advantage disappears.
- **Strategy**: Any "did this market move / pricing change / policy work?" claim built retrospectively from observational data should first pass the target-trial test — it disciplines what estimand is even being claimed and exposes whether the comparison is confounded or time-misaligned, raising a retrospective story from anecdote toward a contestable causal claim (cf. correlation-vs-causation, `references/minerva-hcs.md`, `references/i-ops-analytics-and-metrics.md`).
- **2026**: TTE is now the default scaffold for real-world-evidence causal claims: 2024–2026 saw FDA/EMA guidance encouraging fit-for-purpose RWE, an *Annals of Internal Medicine* framework paper (2024) on when the target trial is and isn't helpful, R tooling (`TrialEmulation`), and the EU TARGET-EU consortium running 10 emulated trials (due mid-2026). Active fronts: clone-censor-weight and sequential-trial designs for time-varying treatment, and emerging LLM/EHR-extraction pipelines for operationalizing protocols at scale — promising but not yet a substitute for careful protocol specification.
